Primer

A self-contained mathematical overview of Neuroalgebraic Geometry.

Neuromanifolds

Definition (Neuromanifold). Let $X$ (inputs), $Y$ (outputs) and $\Theta$ (parameters) be sets. A model is a family of functions $f_\theta \colon X \to Y$ indexed by $\theta \in \Theta$. Its parametrization map is $$ \varphi \colon \Theta \to Y^X, \qquad \theta \mapsto f_\theta, $$ where $Y^X$ is the set of functions $X \to Y$. The neuromanifold of the model is the image $\M = \varphi(\Theta) \subseteq Y^X$, i.e., the set of functions expressible by the model.

Neuromanifolds are central objects in the theory of deep learning, and have been analyzed from several mathematical perspectives. In statistical learning theory, they are known as hypothesis spaces, and their capacity controls the amount of data needed to learn (see Dimension, degree, sample complexity). In information geometry, where the name comes from, they are studied via Riemannian geometry, equipped with the Fisher information metric (see, e.g., Amari et al., 2001). Despite the terminology, neuromanifolds are not manifolds in general: they have singularities, which affect learning (see Singularities).

Models can be composed: the composition of $f_\theta \colon X \to Y$, $\theta \in \Theta$, and $g_{\theta'} \colon Y \to Z$, $\theta' \in \Theta'$, is the model $g_{\theta'} \circ f_\theta \colon X \to Z$ with parameters $(\theta, \theta') \in \Theta \times \Theta'$. This gives the category $\mathbf{Para}$ of parametrized functions (Fong et al., 2019), whose objects are sets and whose morphisms are models, up to bijections of parameter spaces. Compositionality is at the heart of deep learning: a deep model is a composition $f^{(L)} \circ \cdots \circ f^{(1)}$ of a sequence of models, called layers.

Algebraic models

Definition (Algebraic model). Let $\K$ be an infinite field. A model is algebraic if $X = \K^{\din}$, $Y = \K^{\dout}$, $\Theta = \K^{\dpar}$, and $f_\theta(x)$ is a polynomial in $(\theta, x)$.

Let $k$ be the degree of $f_\theta(x)$ in $x$, and denote by $\K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_{\le k}$ the space of polynomials of degree at most $k$ in $\din$ variables. Then $\M$ lives in the finite-dimensional space $$ \K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_{\le k}^{\dout} \cong \K^N, \qquad N = \dout \binom{\din + k}{k}. $$ Moreover, expanding $f_\theta(x) = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} c_\alpha(\theta)\, x^\alpha$ with $c_\alpha \in \K[\theta]^{\dout}$ shows that the parametrization is a polynomial map \begin{equation}\label{eq:parametrization} \varphi \colon \K^{\dpar} \to \K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_{\le k}^{\dout}, \qquad \theta \mapsto f_\theta. \end{equation}

From now on, we fix a finite-dimensional linear space $V \subseteq Y^X$ containing $\M$, called the ambient space. It can be larger than $\K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_{\le k}^{\dout}$, or as small as $\operatorname{Span}(\M)$.

Finite-dimensionality is very special to polynomials. For instance, let $\sigma \colon \R \to \R$ be continuous. Pinkus (1999) proves that the functions $x \mapsto \sigma(w \cdot x + b)$, for $w \in \R^{\din}$ and $b \in \R$, span a finite-dimensional space if and only if $\sigma$ is a polynomial. Hence, for a perceptron () with a non-polynomial activation, $\M$ is not contained in any finite-dimensional space. However, finite-dimensionality is also an obstruction to universal approximation (see Universal approximation).

Recall that a subset of $\K^N$ is constructible if it is a finite union of sets of the form $\{g_1 = \cdots = g_s = 0,\ h \neq 0\}$, and that a subset of $\R^N$ is semi-algebraic if it is a finite union of sets of the form $\{g_1 = \cdots = g_s = 0,\ h_1 > 0, \ldots, h_t > 0\}$, where the $g_i$ and $h_j$ are polynomials.

Theorem Let $\M$ be the neuromanifold of an algebraic model. If $\K$ is algebraically closed, then $\M$ is constructible. If $\K = \R$, then $\M$ is semi-algebraic.

Proof.

By \eqref{eq:parametrization}, $\M$ is the image of a polynomial map $\K^{\dpar} \to \K^N$. Such images are constructible when $\K$ is algebraically closed, by Chevalley's theorem (Hartshorne, 1977, Exercise II.3.19), and semi-algebraic when $\K = \R$, by the Tarski–Seidenberg theorem (Bochnak et al., 1998).

In algebraic geometry, it is of course convenient to deal with actual algebraic varieties. We therefore define the neurovariety $\overline{\M}$ as the closure of $\M$ in the Zariski topology of $V$.

Examples

Example (Perceptrons). Let $\sigma \colon \K \to \K$ be a function, the activation. A perceptron is the model $$ f_\theta(x) = \sigma(W x + b), \qquad \theta = (W, b) \in \K^{\dout \times \din} \times \K^{\dout}, $$ i.e., an affine map followed by the activation, applied component-wise. If $\sigma$ is a polynomial, the perceptron is algebraic. A deep model whose layers are perceptrons is a multilayer perceptron (MLP); usually, the last layer has no activation: $$ f_\theta(x) = W_L\, \sigma\big(W_{L-1} \cdots \sigma(W_1 x + b_1) \cdots + b_{L-1}\big) + b_L, $$ where $W_i \in \K^{d_i \times d_{i-1}}$ and $b_i \in \K^{d_i}$, for widths $d_0 = \din, d_1, \ldots, d_L = \dout$. Since compositions of algebraic models are algebraic, an MLP with polynomial activation is algebraic.

Two cases are of particular interest. In both, the biases vanish: $b_1 = \cdots = b_L = 0$.

  • Linear networks: $\sigma = \mathrm{id}$. Then $f_\theta(x) = W_L \cdots W_1 x$ is linear, and we take $V = \K^{\dout \times \din}$, the space of linear maps $\K^{\din} \to \K^{\dout}$. The product $W_L \cdots W_1$ has rank at most the smallest width $d_{\min} = \min\{d_0, \ldots, d_L\}$, and every matrix of rank at most $d_{\min}$ factors in this way. Thus $\M$ is the determinantal variety of $\dout \times \din$ matrices of rank at most $d_{\min}$, of dimension $d_{\min}(\din + \dout - d_{\min})$. It is already Zariski closed, being the vanishing locus of the minors of size $d_{\min} + 1$: $\overline{\M} = \M$.
  • Shallow monomial networks: $L = 2$ and $\sigma(t) = t^r$. Then $$ f_\theta(x) = W_2\, \sigma(W_1 x) = \sum_{j=1}^{d_1} v_j\, (w_j \cdot x)^r, $$ where $w_j \in \K^{\din}$ is the $j$-th row of $W_1$ and $v_j \in \K^{\dout}$ the $j$-th column of $W_2$, and we take $V = \K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_r^{\dout}$, the space of homogeneous polynomial maps of degree $r$. If $\dout = 1$, $\overline{\M}$ is the affine cone over the $d_1$-th secant variety of the Veronese variety $\nu_r(\mathbb{P}^{\din - 1}) \subseteq \mathbb{P}(V)$ of $r$-th powers of linear forms, i.e., the closure of the set of linear combinations of $d_1$ such powers. In general, $\overline{\M}$ is the affine cone over the $d_1$-th secant variety of the Segre–Veronese variety $\mathbb{P}^{\dout - 1} \times \nu_r(\mathbb{P}^{\din - 1}) \subseteq \mathbb{P}(V)$, whose affine cone consists of the single neurons $x \mapsto v\, (w \cdot x)^r$. Unlike in the linear case, $\M$ need not be closed. For instance, if $\dout = 1$, $d_1 = 2 \le \din$ and $r \ge 3$, then $$ x_1^{r-1} x_2 = \lim_{\lambda \to 0} \frac{(x_1 + \lambda x_2)^r - x_1^r}{r \lambda} $$ lies in $\overline{\M}$ but not in $\M$: a linear combination of two $r$-th powers of linear forms is either a multiple of an $r$-th power or has no repeated linear factor.

Example (Weight sharing). The weights of some edges of a network can be tied together. Formally, let $\Theta = \K^E$ for a finite set $E$ (e.g., of edges), and let $\sim$ be an equivalence relation on $E$. Imposing $\theta_e = \theta_{e'}$ whenever $e \sim e'$ yields a model with parameter space $\K^{E/{\sim}}$. In ML, this is called parameter sharing; in algebraic geometry, it is a quotient: the coordinate ring of $\K^{E/{\sim}}$ is $\K[\theta_e : e \in E]/(\theta_e - \theta_{e'} : e \sim e')$.

For instance, a convolutional layer, as in convolutional neural networks (CNNs), is a perceptron in which each output $y_i$ is connected only to the inputs $x_i, \ldots, x_{i+m-1}$, and edges related by a translation share their weight: $$ y_i = \sigma\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j\, x_{i+j-1} + b\Big), \qquad 1 \le i \le \dout = \din - m + 1. $$ Its parameters are the kernel $w \in \K^m$ and the bias $b \in \K$.

Example (Skip connections). Let $f_\theta \colon \K^{\din} \to \K^{\dout}$ be a model. If $\din = \dout$, then $x \mapsto f_\theta(x) + x$ is again a model, with the same parameters, and it is algebraic if $f$ is. This is called a skip or residual connection. It is often applied layer-wise: replacing each layer $f^{(i)}$ of a deep model by $\mathrm{id} + f^{(i)}$ gives $$ (\mathrm{id} + f^{(L)}) \circ \cdots \circ (\mathrm{id} + f^{(1)}), $$ as in residual networks (ResNets). If $\din \neq \dout$, a skip connection can still be added by fixing a linear map $\pi \colon \K^{\din} \to \K^{\dout}$: the residual model is then $x \mapsto f_\theta(x) + \pi(x)$.

Submodels

Definition (Morphism). Let $\varphi \colon \Theta \to Y^X$ and $\varphi' \colon \Theta' \to Y^X$ be the parametrization maps of two models $f_\theta$ and $f'_{\theta'}$ with the same inputs $X$ and outputs $Y$. A morphism from the second model to the first is a map $\iota \colon \Theta' \to \Theta$ such that the triangle $$ \begin{array}{c@{\,}c@{\,}c} \Theta' & \xrightarrow{\iota} & \Theta \\[4pt] & \kern-0.75em\llap{\lower{6pt}{\scriptstyle\varphi'}\kern-6pt}\style{display:inline-block;transform:rotate(45deg)}{\xrightarrow{\hphantom{MMM}}}\kern-0.7em\style{display:inline-block;transform:rotate(-45deg)}{\xleftarrow{\hphantom{MMM}}}\rlap{\kern-6pt\lower{6pt}{\scriptstyle\varphi}}\kern-0.75em & \\[2pt] & Y^X & \end{array} $$ commutes, i.e., $\varphi \circ \iota = \varphi'$, or equivalently $f_{\iota(\theta')} = f'_{\theta'}$ for all $\theta' \in \Theta'$.

If models whose parameter spaces are in bijection are not identified, $\mathbf{Para}$ becomes a bicategory, whose 2-morphisms are precisely the morphisms of models, also called reparametrizations (Cruttwell et al., 2022).

Definition (Submodel). A submodel of a model is an injective morphism into it. Equivalently, up to bijection, a submodel of a model with parametrization map $\varphi \colon \Theta \to Y^X$ is a subset $\Theta' \subseteq \Theta$, with parametrization map the restriction $\varphi|_{\Theta'}$.

Submodels of neural networks are central in questions related to (implicit) bias and generalization. By Occam's razor, among the solutions to a given task, we should prefer the simplest one, which, in our setting, means one lying in the smallest submodel.

Example (Subnetworks). The main examples of submodels of neural networks are subnetworks. The precise notion depends on the architecture and varies somewhat across the literature but, roughly speaking, a subnetwork is a submodel whose parameters have some “neurons” inactive or redundant, so that its functions are also computed by a smaller network, with these neurons removed. For instance, consider an MLP () with activation $\sigma$, and let $\Sigma \subseteq \K \setminus \{0\}$ be the homogeneity group of $\sigma$, i.e., the group of the nonzero scalars $\lambda$ such that $\sigma(\lambda t) = c\, \sigma(t)$ for all $t \in \K$ and some $c \in \K$. We have $\Sigma = \K \setminus \{0\}$ if $\sigma(t) = t^r$ is a monomial, while $\Sigma = \{1\}$ for a generic polynomial $\sigma$. Let $0 < j < L$ and $1 \le i \le d_j$. The $i$-th neuron in layer $j$ is:

  • inactive if its incoming or outgoing weights vanish, i.e., $W_j[i,:] = 0$ or $W_{j+1}[:,i] = 0$;
  • redundant if $W_j[i,:] \neq 0$ and $(W_j[i,:], b_j[i]) = \lambda\, (W_j[k,:], b_j[k])$ for some $\lambda \in \Sigma$ and some $1 \le k \le d_j$ with $k \neq i$.

Such neurons can be removed without changing $f_\theta$, which therefore lies in the neuromanifold of a smaller network, embedded in $\M$. Thus, the parameters at which given neurons are inactive or redundant form subnetworks.

For MLPs with skip connections (), i.e., ResNets, subnetworks are defined only by vanishing incoming weights. If $W_j[i,:] = 0$, the $i$-th neuron in layer $j$ only adds the constant $\sigma(b_j[i])$ to the $i$-th coordinate, which can be absorbed by the subsequent biases. The neuron can then be removed by introducing a projection: the remaining neurons of the layer form the residual model $x \mapsto f'(x) + \pi(x)$, where $f'$ is the perceptron without the $i$-th neuron and $\pi \colon \K^{d_j} \to \K^{d_j - 1}$ forgets the $i$-th coordinate, while $x_i$ is passed on unchanged. The other conditions do not define removable neurons, since the skip connection still lets the information of the $i$-th coordinate flow to the subsequent layers.

Learning as distance optimization

Ideally, the goal of machine learning is to approximate a target function $f^* \in V$ by a function in the neuromanifold $\M$. We do not have access to $f^*$: it is a “Platonic” object. This is the setting of supervised learning. Other learning paradigms, such as generative modelling and reinforcement learning, either reduce to it or can be formalized similarly, with some modifications.

If we had access to $f^*$, we could simply minimize a distance between functions, i.e., find the points of $\M$ closest to $f^*$. For $\K = \R$, a natural choice is the squared distance $\|f - f^*\|^2$ induced by an inner product on $V$, for example the $L^2$ product $$ \langle f, g \rangle = \int_X \langle f(x), g(x) \rangle \, d\mu(x) $$ for a probability measure $\mu$ on $X$. For algebraic models, where $V$ consists of polynomial maps, a standard inner product is the Bombieri–Weyl one, defined on monomials by $\langle x^\alpha, x^\beta \rangle = \delta_{\alpha \beta} \binom{k}{\alpha}^{-1}$ (and extended to homogeneous polynomials by homogenization, and to polynomial maps by summing over the components).

Thus, ideally, machine learning is a distance minimization problem over the neuromanifold, with respect to a distance induced by an inner product on $V$. This is one of the problems at the heart of metric algebraic geometry (Breiding et al., 2024), a field that studies the interplay between real algebraic varieties and a distance on their ambient space.

Datasets and losses

In practice, however, we do not have access to $f^*$, but only to finitely many samples from it, which form a dataset.

Definition (Dataset). A dataset is a finite subset $D \subseteq X \times Y$, whose elements $(x_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \ldots, n$, are called datapoints.

The datapoints are assumed to lie on the graph of $f^*$, i.e., $y_i = f^*(x_i)$. Alternatively, as is typical in statistical learning theory, one can dispense with $f^*$ and think of $D$ probabilistically, as sampled from an unknown probability distribution over $X \times Y$; this accounts, for instance, for noise in the labels. For $\K = \R$, the role of $f^*$ is then played by the regression function $x \mapsto \mathbb{E}[y \mid x]$ (Cucker and Smale, 2002).

The model then learns by minimizing a loss $L \colon V \to \R$ over $\M$ that depends only on $D$ (in practice, $L \circ \varphi$ over $\Theta$, e.g., by gradient descent). A typical template is $$ L(f) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell(f(x_i), y_i), $$ where $\ell \colon Y \times Y \to \R$. A standard choice, for $\K = \R$, is the mean squared error (MSE), where $\ell(y, y') = \|y - y'\|^2$ for a norm on $Y = \R^{\dout}$ induced by an inner product. Since $f(x_i)$ depends linearly on $f$, the MSE is a polynomial on $V$, quadratic in fact. It is a data-driven discretization, or statistical approximation, of the ideal loss for the $L^2$ product: if $y_i = f^*(x_i)$, it is the same loss with $\mu$ replaced by the empirical measure $\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta_{x_i}$, and if the $x_i$ are sampled independently from $\mu$, it converges to $\|f - f^*\|^2$ as $n \to \infty$, by the law of large numbers. We will often focus on the MSE in this primer.

Finally, datapoints and datasets can be viewed geometrically, as affine subspaces of $V$.

Definition (Interpolating subspace). The interpolating subspace of a dataset $D$ is $$ H_D = \{ f \in V : f(x) = y \text{ for all } (x, y) \in D \}. $$

The evaluation $\mathrm{ev}_x \colon V \to Y$, $f \mapsto f(x)$, is linear. Hence, for a datapoint, $H_{(x, y)} = \mathrm{ev}_x^{-1}(y)$ is an affine subspace, of codimension $\dim Y = \dout$ whenever $\mathrm{ev}_x$ is surjective (e.g., when $V$ contains the constant maps). For a dataset, $H_D$ is the intersection of the $H_{(x_i, y_i)}$, an affine subspace that may be empty. The functions in $\M \cap H_D$ are those expressible by the model that interpolate $D$. For $\K = \R$, $H_D$ coincides exactly with the kernel of the MSE (this holds for any positive definite $\ell$), so that, whenever $\M \cap H_D$ is non-empty, its points are the global minima of the MSE over $\M$.

Interpolating subspaces are not arbitrary affine subspaces of $V$, and not even generic ones. For instance, if $\dout = 1$ and $V = \K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_{\le k}$, then $f(x) = \langle c, \nu_k(x) \rangle$, where $c$ is the vector of coefficients of $f$ and $\nu_k(x) = (x^\alpha)_{|\alpha| \le k}$ is the Veronese map. Thus $H_{(x, y)} = \{ c : \langle c, \nu_k(x) \rangle = y \}$ is defined by a linear form on the Veronese variety, of dimension $\din$, rather than anywhere in $V^*$, of dimension $\binom{\din + k}{k}$. In particular, results on generic linear sections of $\M$ do not apply directly. However, in other paradigms a datapoint may involve several inputs at once, and impose more general affine conditions: for instance, $f(x) = f(x')$ for a positive pair $(x, x')$ in contrastive learning, or the Bellman equation $f(s, a) = r + \gamma f(s', a')$ for a transition $(s, a) \to (s', a')$ with reward $r$ and discount $\gamma$ in reinforcement learning. It is therefore worth, at times, abstracting to arbitrary affine subspaces of $V$.

Universal approximation

A natural question is which functions a model can express and, in particular, whether every function can be approximated by large enough models of a given class. For MLPs, the answer is the celebrated universal approximation theorem: if the activation $\sigma \colon \R \to \R$ is continuous and not a polynomial, then MLPs with a single hidden layer can approximate any continuous function on a compact set arbitrarily well, provided that they are wide enough (Cybenko, 1989; Hornik et al., 1989; Leshno et al., 1993).

Algebraic models behave differently. If $\sigma$ is a polynomial of degree $r$, an MLP with $L$ layers has degree at most $r^{L-1}$ in $x$, whatever its widths. Hence, as the widths vary, its neuromanifold stays in the same ambient space $V = \K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_{\le r^{L-1}}^{\dout}$, and, since $V$ is finite-dimensional, universal approximation as above cannot hold. Within $V$, however, MLPs with a polynomial activation are as expressive as possible: at fixed depth, the union over all widths of their neuromanifolds is the whole ambient space.

Lemma Let $\K$ be a field of characteristic $0$, and let $\sigma \in \K[t]$ have degree $r$. Then the translates $\sigma(t + b)$, for $b \in \K$, span $\K[t]_{\le r}$.

Proof.

The translates lie in $\K[t]_{\le r}$, and their span contains the finite differences $\Delta^j \sigma$, where $\Delta p(t) = p(t + 1) - p(t)$. In characteristic $0$, $\Delta$ lowers the degree of a non-constant polynomial by exactly one, so that $\Delta^j \sigma$ has degree $r - j$ for $j = 0, \ldots, r$. Hence these finite differences form a basis of $\K[t]_{\le r}$.

Theorem Let $\K$ be a field of characteristic $0$, and let $\sigma \in \K[t]$ have degree $r \ge 1$. Then the union over all widths of the neuromanifolds of MLPs with $L$ layers and activation $\sigma$ is $\K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_{\le r^{L-1}}^{\dout}$.

Proof.

Let $\mathcal{F}_L$ be the set of functions $\K^{\din} \to \K$ computed by MLPs with $L$ layers and one output, of any widths. Placing such MLPs side by side, on the same input, shows that finitely many elements of $\mathcal{F}_L$ are computed by a single MLP with several outputs, that $\mathcal{F}_L$ is a vector space containing the constants, and that $\mathcal{F}_{L+1}$ is spanned by the constants and the functions $\sigma(u)$, for $u \in \mathcal{F}_L$. It thus suffices to show that $\mathcal{F}_L = \K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_{\le r^{L-1}}$, by induction on $L$. For $L = 1$, $\mathcal{F}_1$ consists of the affine functions. Assume the claim for $L$. Since $\sigma$ has degree $r$, $\mathcal{F}_{L+1} \subseteq \K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_{\le r^L}$. Conversely, by , $t^r = \sum_j c_j \sigma(t + b_j)$ for some $c_j, b_j \in \K$, so that $u^r = \sum_j c_j \sigma(u + b_j) \in \mathcal{F}_{L+1}$ for every $u \in \mathcal{F}_L$. By the polarization identity $$ r!\, 2^r\, u_1 \cdots u_r = \sum_{\varepsilon \in \{\pm 1\}^r} \varepsilon_1 \cdots \varepsilon_r \, (\varepsilon_1 u_1 + \cdots + \varepsilon_r u_r)^r, $$ $\mathcal{F}_{L+1}$ then contains every product of $r$ elements of $\mathcal{F}_L$. Since every monomial of degree at most $r^L$ is a product of $r$ monomials of degree at most $r^{L-1}$, these products span $\K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]_{\le r^L}$.

If the depth is allowed to grow, the bound on the degree disappears.

Corollary In the setting of , if $r \ge 2$, every polynomial map $\K^{\din} \to \K^{\dout}$ is computed by an MLP with activation $\sigma$, of sufficient depth and width.

Proof.

Since $r \ge 2$, every polynomial map has degree at most $r^{L-1}$ for $L$ large enough, so the claim follows from .

Over $\K = \R$, together with the Weierstrass approximation theorem, this shows that MLPs with a polynomial activation of degree at least $2$ are universal approximators, provided that the depth is allowed to grow. Weierstrass-type theorems hold for many function spaces and norms beyond the uniform norm on continuous functions, such as $L^p$ and Sobolev spaces, each with its own error bounds (Pinkus, 2005; DeVore and Lorentz, 1993).

Dimension, degree, sample complexity

The dimension and the degree are two fundamental invariants of an algebraic variety. Recall that the degree of an irreducible affine variety $Z \subseteq \K^n$, for $\K$ algebraically closed, is the number of points of its intersection with a generic affine subspace of complementary dimension; for a reducible variety, it is the sum of the degrees of its irreducible components. Thus, while the dimension counts the degrees of freedom of $Z$, the degree measures how far $Z$ is from being linear: the irreducible varieties of degree $1$ are exactly the affine subspaces. By the dimension and the degree of a model, or of $\M$, we mean those of the neurovariety $\overline{\M}$, which is irreducible since $\Theta = \K^{\dpar}$ is. For $\K = \R$, the degree is computed over $\C$: we pass to the complex model, whose neurovariety is the complex Zariski closure of $\M$, of the same dimension.

In machine learning, the dimension and the degree of the neuromanifold are related to a natural question: how much data a model needs. Informally, the sample complexity of a model is the number of datapoints $|D|$ needed to learn the target function $f^*$, up to a given accuracy and with a given confidence. The link passes through another invariant of $\M$: its covering numbers, which measure its size at every scale. In statistical learning theory, they control the sample complexity (Cucker and Smale, 2002): their logarithm plays an analogous role to the Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension in binary classification, and bounds the number of datapoints needed for the empirical loss to approximate the population loss uniformly over the model.

Definition (Covering number). Let $Z$ be a metric space, and let $\varepsilon > 0$. The covering number $\mathcal{N}_\varepsilon(Z)$ is the least number of closed balls of radius $\varepsilon$, centered at points of $Z$, that cover $Z$. Its logarithm is the metric entropy of $Z$.

Hence $\mathcal{N}_\varepsilon(Z)$ is finite for every $\varepsilon > 0$ if and only if $Z$ is totally bounded, e.g., a bounded subset of $\R^N$, and covering numbers quantify total boundedness.

Bounding the covering numbers of algebraic varieties in terms of their fundamental invariants, such as the dimension and the degree, is a long-standing endeavor in metric algebraic geometry. It goes back to Kolmogorov and Vitushkin (Vitushkin, 1955; Kolmogorov and Tikhomirov, 1959), passes through the book of Yomdin and Comte (2004), and leads to the recent bounds of Zhang and Kileel (2025). Over $\R$, it is also related to Weyl's formula for the volume of tubes around a variety (see, e.g., Basu and Lerario, 2023). The covering bound below rests on the following volume estimates for complex varieties.

Lemma (Lelong, Thie, Stoll). Let $Z \subseteq \C^N$ be an irreducible variety of dimension $m$ and degree $p$. Let $\mathcal{H}^{2m}$ be the $2m$-dimensional Hausdorff measure, $\omega_{2m} = \pi^m / m!$ the volume of the unit ball of $\C^m = \R^{2m}$, and $B(a, r) \subseteq \C^N$ the open ball of center $a$ and radius $r$. For $a \in \C^N$, the function $$ \tau_a(r) = \frac{\mathcal{H}^{2m}\big(Z \cap B(a, r)\big)}{\omega_{2m}\, r^{2m}}, \qquad r > 0, $$ is non-decreasing. If $a \in Z$, then $\tau_a(r) \to \operatorname{mult}_a Z \ge 1$ as $r \to 0$, and in all cases $\tau_a(r) \to p$ as $r \to \infty$. Consequently, for all $r > 0$, $\mathcal{H}^{2m}(Z \cap B(a, r)) \ge \omega_{2m}\, r^{2m}$ if $a \in Z$, and $\mathcal{H}^{2m}(Z \cap B(a, r)) \le p\, \omega_{2m}\, r^{2m}$ for all $a \in \C^N$.

Proof sketch.

By the Lelong–Jensen formula, for $0 \lt s \lt r$, $$ \tau_a(r) - \tau_a(s) = \int_{Z \cap \{s \lt |z - a| \lt r\}} \big(dd^c \log |z - a|^2\big)^m \ge 0, $$ where $dd^c \log |z - a|^2$ is the pullback of the Fubini–Study form, of mass $1$ on lines, under $z \mapsto [z - a] \in \mathbb{P}^{N-1}$. Hence $\tau_a$ is non-decreasing (Lelong, 1957), and $\tau_a(0^+) = \operatorname{mult}_a Z$ (Thie, 1967), where $\operatorname{mult}_a Z = 0$ if $a \notin Z$. As $s \to 0$ and $r \to \infty$, Crofton's formula on $\mathbb{P}^{N-1}$ identifies the integral with the number of points of $Z \cap L$ other than $a$, for a generic affine subspace $L \ni a$ of codimension $m$. This number is $p - \operatorname{mult}_a Z$, so that $\tau_a(\infty) = p$ (Stoll, 1964). See Chirka (1989) for details.

Let $V$ be real, of dimension $N$, and let $\M \subseteq V$ be semi-algebraic of dimension $m$, for instance a neuromanifold (), and let $p$ be the degree of its Zariski closure in $V_\C = V \otimes_\R \C$. If $\M$ is a neuromanifold, then $m$ and $p$ are its dimension and degree.

Theorem Fix a scalar product on $V$, with closed unit ball $B$ and the induced distance. For every $\varepsilon > 0$, $$ \mathcal{N}_\varepsilon(\M \cap B) \le p \Big(1 + \frac{2}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{2m}, $$ The same bound holds for $T(\M) \cap B_U$, for every linear map $T \colon V \to U$ to a Euclidean space $U$ with closed unit ball $B_U$; for instance, for the image of $\M$ in $L^2(\mu)$, for every probability measure $\mu$ on the inputs under which the functions in $V$ are square-integrable, e.g., an empirical measure.

Proof.

Let $Z \subseteq V_\C$ be the Zariski closure of $\M$, with irreducible components $Z_1, \ldots, Z_s$ of dimensions $m_j$ and degrees $p_j$. Then $m_j \le \dim_\C Z = m$ and $\sum_j p_j = p$. Extend the scalar product to a Hermitian one on $V_\C$, so that $\|u + iv\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2$ and $V \subseteq V_\C$ isometrically. In orthonormal coordinates, $V_\C = \C^N$, and dimension and degree are unchanged. Partition $\M \cap B = S_1 \sqcup \cdots \sqcup S_s$, with $S_j \subseteq Z_j$. Let $P \subseteq S_j$ be finite and $\varepsilon$-separated, i.e., $\|f - g\| > \varepsilon$ for distinct $f, g \in P$. The balls $B(f, \varepsilon/2) \subseteq \C^N$, for $f \in P$, are pairwise disjoint, centered on $Z_j$, and contained in $B(0, 1 + \varepsilon/2)$. By , $$ \begin{aligned} |P|\, \omega_{2m_j} \Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big)^{2m_j} &\le \sum_{f \in P} \mathcal{H}^{2m_j}\big(Z_j \cap B(f, \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})\big) \\ &\le \mathcal{H}^{2m_j}\big(Z_j \cap B(0, 1 + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})\big) \le p_j\, \omega_{2m_j} \Big(1 + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big)^{2m_j}, \end{aligned} $$ so that $|P| \le p_j (1 + 2/\varepsilon)^{2m_j}$. Hence a maximal $\varepsilon$-separated subset of $S_j$ exists, is finite, and is an $\varepsilon$-net of $S_j$ with centers in $S_j$. Summing over $j$, and using $m_j \le m$ and $\sum_j p_j = p$, gives the bound. For the last claim, extend $T$ to a $\C$-linear map. The complex Zariski closure of $T(\M)$ is $\overline{T(Z)}$, of dimension at most $m$ and degree at most $p$, since linear maps do not increase the degree (Heintz, 1983). Apply the first part to $T(\M) \subseteq U$.

The bound involves neither $N$ nor the number $\dpar$ of parameters, only $m \le \dpar$ and $\log p$. Over $\R$, there is no analogue of the lower bound of : $\{x^2 + y^2 = 0\}$ is a point, and $\{x^2 + y^2 = \eta\}$ an arbitrarily small circle. Real proofs therefore count connected components of slices, through the Petrovskii–Oleinik–Milnor–Thom bounds on Betti numbers, or bound volumes of tubes (Yomdin and Comte, 2004; Zhang and Kileel, 2025), which brings in $N$ and the degrees of the defining equations. The proof of instead transplants Gromov's packing argument for the entropy of holomorphic maps (Gromov, 2003) to the complexification of $\M$: complex varieties are calibrated, hence minimal, so that monotonicity provides the lower bound for free, at the price of the factor $2$.

The form $\log p + \Theta(m) \log(1/\varepsilon)$ of the bound cannot be improved. The $p$ parallel affine $m$-planes $\frac{1}{2} e_j + \R^m$, $j = 1, \ldots, p$, in $\R^p \times \R^m$ form a variety of degree $p$ that needs at least $p\,(c/\varepsilon)^m$ balls, for an absolute constant $c > 0$ and $\varepsilon \lt 1/4$. The term $\log p$ is needed for irreducible varieties too: the trigonometric moment curve $\gamma(t) = k^{-1/2}(\cos t, \sin t, \ldots, \cos kt, \sin kt)$ is a rational normal curve of degree $p = 2k$ on the unit sphere, and the $k$ points $\gamma(2\pi j/k)$ are orthonormal, so that $\mathcal{N}_\varepsilon \ge p/2$ for $\varepsilon \lt 1/\sqrt{2}$.

For learning, let $\dout = 1$, let the inputs lie in a set $\mathcal{X} \subseteq X$, and let $\mathcal{F} = \{f \in \M : |f(x)| \le 1 \text{ for all } x \in \mathcal{X}\}$. Let $(x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)$ be sampled i.i.d. from a probability distribution $P$ on $\mathcal{X} \times [-1, 1]$, and write $L_P(f) = \mathbb{E}_P (f(x) - y)^2$ for the population loss and $L_D(f) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i (f(x_i) - y_i)^2$ for the MSE of the resulting dataset $D$.

Corollary There is an absolute constant $C$ such that, with probability at least $1 - \delta$, $$ \sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \big| L_P(f) - L_D(f) \big| \le C \sqrt{\frac{m + \log p + \log(2/\delta)}{n}}. $$ In particular, $n = O\big(\varepsilon^{-2} (m + \log p + \log(1/\delta))\big)$ datapoints suffice for empirical risk minimization over $\mathcal{F}$ to be $\varepsilon$-optimal in $\mathcal{F}$.

Proof.

Fix $S = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$, and let $T_S \colon V \to \R^n$, $f \mapsto (f(x_i))_i$, which is linear. With $\|u\|_S = (\frac{1}{n} \sum_i u_i^2)^{1/2}$, we have $\mathcal{F}|_S \subseteq T_S(\M) \cap \{\|u\|_S \le 1\}$, so that gives, uniformly in $S$ and $n$, $$ \log \mathcal{N}_t\big(\mathcal{F}|_S, \|\cdot\|_S\big) \le \log p + 2m \log(1 + 4/t), \qquad t > 0, $$ where the $4$ comes from passing to the subset $\mathcal{F}|_S$. Dudley's entropy integral (Dudley, 1967) bounds the empirical Rademacher complexity: $$ \widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{F}) \lesssim \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \int_0^1 \sqrt{\log p + 2m \log(1 + 4/t)}\, dt \lesssim \sqrt{\frac{m + \log p}{n}}. $$ The loss $(\hat{y}, y) \mapsto (\hat{y} - y)^2$ takes values in $[0, 4]$ and is $4$-Lipschitz in $\hat{y} \in [-1, 1]$, so that the contraction lemma and the standard symmetrization and bounded-differences argument (Bartlett and Mendelson, 2002) give the claim. A uniform deviation of at most $\varepsilon/2$ makes empirical risk minimization $\varepsilon$-optimal.

Identifiability

A classical question about a model, known as identifiability, is determining the parameters that define the same function. In our language, such parameters $\theta$ form the fiber $\varphi^{-1}(f_\theta)$ of the parametrization map, and identifiability asks for a description of the fibers. As we shall see, the geometry of the fibers plays a role in several aspects, from expressivity to optimization.

For algebraic models, fibers are well behaved. The degree of $\varphi$ is the degree of its graph $\Gamma_\varphi = \{(\theta, f_\theta) : \theta \in \Theta\} \subseteq \Theta \times V$. Explicitly, $\deg \varphi$ is the number of solutions $\theta \in \Theta$ of $\dpar$ generic equations $\ell_i(\theta) + \ell'_i(f_\theta) = c_i$, where $\ell_i$ and $\ell'_i$ are linear forms on $\Theta$ and $V$, and $c_i \in \K$. By Bézout's theorem, $\deg \varphi \le (\deg_\theta f_\theta)^{\dpar}$, where $\deg_\theta$ denotes the degree in $\theta$.

Theorem (Generic fiber). There is a dense Zariski-open subset $U \subseteq \overline{\M}$, contained in $\M$, such that the fibers $\varphi^{-1}(f)$, for $f \in U$, are equidimensional, with the same dimension and the same degree. Moreover, for $f \in U$:

  • (Fiber-dimension) $\dim \M = \dim \Theta - \dim \varphi^{-1}(f)$.
  • (Bézout) $\deg \M \le \deg \varphi \,/\, \deg \varphi^{-1}(f)$.
Proof.

The statements on the generic fiber follow from the theorem on the dimension of fibers and from generic flatness (Hartshorne, 1977, Exercise II.3.22 and Theorems III.9.7 and III.9.9). For the inequality, let $m = \dim \M$ and $e = \dpar - m$, and let $L = A \times B \subseteq \Theta \times V$, where $B \subseteq V$ is a generic affine subspace of codimension $m$ and $A \subseteq \Theta$ is a generic affine subspace of codimension $e$. Since $\dim(\overline{\M} \setminus U) \lt m$, $B$ meets $\overline{\M}$ in $\deg \M$ points, all in $U$, and $A$ meets the fiber of each of them, which is equidimensional of dimension $e$, in $\deg \varphi^{-1}(f)$ points. Hence $$ \Gamma_\varphi \cap L = \bigsqcup_{f \in B \cap \overline{\M}} \big(A \cap \varphi^{-1}(f)\big) \times \{f\} $$ consists of $\deg \M \cdot \deg \varphi^{-1}(f)$ points. Each of them is an irreducible component of the intersection of the projective closures of $\Gamma_\varphi$ and $L$, so that, by the Bézout inequality (Fulton, 1998, Example 8.4.6), their number is at most $\deg \Gamma_\varphi \cdot \deg L = \deg \varphi$.

The inequality in is strict in general, because $L$ is not generic: its equations separate $\theta$ and $f$. Its projective closure may therefore meet the projective closure of $\Gamma_\varphi$ at infinity. These contributions at infinity account for the difference between $\deg \varphi$ and $\deg \M \cdot \deg \varphi^{-1}(f)$, in the sense of the refined Bézout theorem (Fulton, 1998, §12.3).

We call $\varphi^{-1}(f)$, for $f \in U$, the generic fiber. In particular, the generic fiber is finite if and only if $\dim \M = \dpar$. In characteristic $0$, $\dim \M$ is the rank of the differential of $\varphi$ at a generic $\theta$, which gives a simple numerical test. By the previous section, relates generic identifiability to sample complexity. Sample complexity is controlled by the dimension and the degree of $\M$, not by the number $\dpar$ of parameters, and by both are smaller when the generic fiber is larger.

Identifiability is also interesting because of its relation to symmetries of the input and output spaces.

Definition (Adjunction). An algebraic model satisfies adjunction if there are a left action of $\mathrm{GL}_{\dout}(\K)$ and a right action of $\mathrm{GL}_{\din}(\K)$ on $\Theta$, written $(T, \theta) \mapsto T\theta$ and $(\theta, T') \mapsto \theta T'$, that commute and such that $$ f_{T\theta T'}(x) = T f_\theta(T'x) $$ for all $T \in \mathrm{GL}_{\dout}(\K)$, $T' \in \mathrm{GL}_{\din}(\K)$, $\theta \in \Theta$ and $x \in X$.

For instance, MLPs satisfy adjunction: $T'$ acts on the first layer by $W_1 \mapsto W_1 T'$, and $T$ acts on the last layer by $(W_L, b_L) \mapsto (TW_L, Tb_L)$.

Let $G$ be a group with representations $\rho_X \colon G \to \mathrm{GL}_{\din}(\K)$ and $\rho_Y \colon G \to \mathrm{GL}_{\dout}(\K)$. Recall that a function $f \colon X \to Y$ is $G$-equivariant if $f(\rho_X(g)x) = \rho_Y(g)f(x)$ for all $g \in G$ and $x \in X$. Equivariance is the central notion of geometric deep learning (Bronstein et al., 2021), which designs architectures that respect the symmetries of the data, such as translations of images for CNNs, or permutations of nodes for graph neural networks. For a model satisfying adjunction, $G$ acts on $\Theta$ by $g \cdot \theta = \rho_Y(g)\, \theta\, \rho_X(g)^{-1}$.

Proposition For a model satisfying adjunction, $f_\theta$ is $G$-equivariant if and only if $G \cdot \theta \subseteq \varphi^{-1}(f_\theta)$, i.e., if and only if the fiber of $f_\theta$ is stable under $G$.

Proof.

By adjunction, $f_{g \cdot \theta}(x) = \rho_Y(g) f_\theta(\rho_X(g)^{-1}x)$. Hence $g \cdot \theta \in \varphi^{-1}(f_\theta)$ if and only if $\rho_Y(g) f_\theta(\rho_X(g)^{-1}x) = f_\theta(x)$ for all $x$, i.e., replacing $x$ by $\rho_X(g)x$, if and only if $f_\theta(\rho_X(g)x) = \rho_Y(g) f_\theta(x)$ for all $x$. For the last claim, if $G \cdot \theta \subseteq \varphi^{-1}(f_\theta)$ and $f_{\theta'} = f_\theta$, then $f_{g \cdot \theta'} = f_{g \cdot \theta} = f_\theta$ by the same formula.

Thus, to understand whether $f_\theta$ is equivariant, we can look at the fiber of $f_\theta$.

Equivariant parameters are special. The $\theta$ for which $f_\theta$ is $G$-equivariant typically form a proper Zariski-closed subset of $\Theta$, which may miss the dense open subset where the model is identifiable.

Identifiability of deep networks

We conclude by describing the fibers of MLPs (). They always contain the following parameters. Permuting the neurons of a hidden layer $j$, i.e., the rows of $W_j$ and $b_j$ and, accordingly, the columns of $W_{j+1}$, does not change $f_\theta$. Neither does rescaling a hidden neuron by $\lambda \in \Sigma$ (), i.e., multiplying its incoming weights and bias by $\lambda$ and its outgoing weights by $c^{-1}$, where $\sigma(\lambda t) = c\, \sigma(t)$. We say that $\theta$ is identifiable if every parameter in its fiber is obtained from $\theta$ by such permutations and rescalings.

To show that, outside of subnetworks, the fibers contain no other parameters, we need the following notion (Borsuk, 1957). The resulting theorem generalizes Corollary 4.1 of Kohn et al. (2026), whose proof we follow.

Definition (Regular map). Let $A$ be a set and $W$ a vector space. A map $g \colon A \to W$ is $s$-regular if, for all $k \le s$ and all pairwise distinct $a_1, \ldots, a_k \in A$, the vectors $g(a_1), \ldots, g(a_k)$ are linearly independent.

Theorem Consider an MLP with $L \ge 2$ layers and activation $\sigma \in \K[t]$ of degree $r$. Let $\overline{d} = \max\{d_1, \ldots, d_{L-1}\}$, and let $P$ be a set of representatives of the $\Sigma$-orbits of the nonconstant polynomials $p \in \K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]$ of degree at most $r^{L-2}$. If the map $P \to \K[x_1, \ldots, x_{\din}]/\K$, $p \mapsto \sigma \circ p + \K$, is $2\overline{d}$-regular, then $\theta \in \Theta$ is identifiable if it is not a subnetwork.

Proof.

Assume that $\theta$ is not a subnetwork, i.e., that no neuron is inactive or redundant at $\theta$, and note that the assumption on $\sigma$ also holds for fewer layers and narrower hidden layers. Let $h^{(j)}$ be the vector of activations of layer $j$, with $h^{(0)} = x$. By induction on $j < L$, the coordinates of $h^{(j)}$ are linearly independent modulo constants: those of $W_{j+1} h^{(j)} + b_{j+1}$, the pre-activations of layer $j + 1$, are then nonconstant polynomials of degree at most $r^j$, which lie in distinct $\Sigma$-orbits, since the rows of $(W_{j+1}, b_{j+1})$ do, so that their images under $\sigma$ are linearly independent modulo constants by assumption.

We now show that $\theta$ is identifiable, by induction on $L$, the case $L = 1$ being trivial. Let $\theta' = (V_1, b'_1, \ldots, V_L, b'_L)$ be in the fiber of $\theta$, and let $p_i$ and $q_i$ be the pre-activations of the $i$-th neuron of layer $L - 1$ at $\theta$ and $\theta'$, respectively. Then $$ \sum_i W_L[:,i]\; \sigma \circ p_i + b_L = \sum_i V_L[:,i]\; \sigma \circ q_i + b'_L, $$ where, by the above, the $p_i$ are nonconstant and lie in distinct $\Sigma$-orbits, and the columns $W_L[:,i]$ are nonzero. Since $\sigma \circ (\lambda p)$ is a multiple of $\sigma \circ p$ for $\lambda \in \Sigma$, the assumption implies that, once the terms with constant $q_i$ are absorbed into the constant term and those in the same $\Sigma$-orbit are combined, all the coefficients vanish. Hence, every $p_i$ lies in the orbit of some $q_{\pi(i)}$, where $\pi$ is injective, hence a permutation, and $W_L[:,i] = c\, V_L[:,\pi(i)]$, where $\sigma \circ q_{\pi(i)} = c\, \sigma \circ p_i$, so that $b_L = b'_L$. Permuting and rescaling the neurons of layer $L - 1$ at $\theta'$, we may thus assume that $q_i = p_i$ and $V_L = W_L$. Then $(W_1, b_1, \ldots, W_{L-1}, b_{L-1})$ and $(V_1, b'_1, \ldots, V_{L-1}, b'_{L-1})$ are parameters of an MLP with $L - 1$ layers that compute the same function $x \mapsto (p_1, \ldots, p_{d_{L-1}})$, and no neuron is inactive or redundant at the former. By induction, they differ by permutations and rescalings, and so do $\theta$ and $\theta'$.

Without biases, the same proof applies under a weaker assumption. If the biases vanish and $\sigma(0) = 0$, all the pre-activations vanish at $0$, so that it suffices to take $P$ among the polynomials with $p(0) = 0$; then $\sigma \circ p$ vanishes at $0$ as well, and the quotient by $\K$ is immaterial. This is the setting of Kohn et al. (2026).

The assumption of holds for monomials and for generic polynomials of large degree $r = \Omega(\overline{d}^2)$. For $\sigma(t) = t^r$, we have $\Sigma = \K \setminus \{0\}$, and the assumption states that the $r$-th powers of at most $s$ pairwise non-proportional polynomials are linearly independent. This follows from the Newman–Slater theorem, i.e., Fermat's last theorem for polynomials (Finkel et al., 2025). For a generic $\sigma$ with $\sigma(0) = 0$, we have $\Sigma = \{1\}$, and the assumption has been proven by Chang and Hendi (2026), as conjectured by Kohn et al. (2026).

On the other hand, subnetworks have higher-dimensional fibers, since one can freely change the incoming weights $W_j[i,:]$ and bias $b_j[i]$ of a neuron whose outgoing weights $W_{j+1}[:,i]$ vanish, as well as the outgoing weights and bias of a neuron whose incoming weights vanish, absorbing its constant output $\sigma(b_j[i])$ into $b_{j+1}$. In particular, if a neuron of layer $j$ is inactive or redundant, then $\dim \varphi^{-1}(f_\theta) \ge \max\{d_{j-1}, d_{j+1}\} + 1$.

Combining with computes the dimension of the neuromanifold of an MLP and bounds its degree. Indeed, a generic $\theta$ is not a subnetwork, so that its fiber is its orbit under permutations and rescalings, which act freely on it. Since permutations are $0$-dimensional, this orbit is finite when $\Sigma$ is, i.e., when $\sigma$ is not a monomial, while for a monomial the rescalings form a torus of dimension $d_1 + \cdots + d_{L-1}$. Moreover, $\deg_\theta f_\theta = 1 + r + \cdots + r^{L-1}$.

Corollary Under the assumptions of , if $\sigma$ is not a monomial, then $\dim \M = \dpar = \sum_{i=1}^{L} d_i (d_{i-1} + 1)$ and $$ \deg \M \le \frac{(1 + r + \cdots + r^{L-1})^{\dpar}}{\prod_{j=1}^{L-1} |\Sigma|^{d_j}\, d_j!}. $$ If $\sigma$ is a monomial, then $\dim \M = \dpar - d_1 - \cdots - d_{L-1}$.

These match, up to constants, the classical bounds through the pseudo-dimension, i.e., the Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension of real-valued classes, which Goldberg and Jerrum (1995) bound in terms of $\dpar$ and $\deg_\theta f_\theta$. They improve those of Zhang and Kileel (2025), which, specialized to polynomial activations, contain additional logarithmic factors in the widths and in the number of samples.

Singularities

Singularities play a central role in algebraic geometry, which is one of the few branches of geometry that can deal with them: the manifolds of differential geometry, for instance, are smooth by definition. Recall that a point $z$ of an irreducible variety $Z \subseteq \K^N$ of dimension $m$ is smooth if the Jacobian matrix $\big(\partial g_i / \partial x_j (z)\big)$ of generators $g_1, \ldots, g_s$ of the ideal of polynomials vanishing on $Z$ has rank $N - m$, i.e., if the tangent space $T_z Z$, the kernel of this matrix, has dimension $m$, the least possible. Otherwise, $z$ is singular. When $\K = \R$ and $Z$ is semi-algebraic, by a singularity of $Z$ we mean a point of $Z$ that is singular in its Zariski closure $\overline{Z}$.

In machine learning, the role of singularities has been highlighted by singular learning theory (Watanabe, 2009), according to which they bias the learning process. One way to see this is via Voronoi cells.

Definition (Voronoi cell). Let $V$ be real, of dimension $N$, with a scalar product, and let $Z \subseteq V$. The Voronoi cell of $f \in Z$ is $$ \mathrm{Vor}_Z(f) = \{ u \in V : \|u - f\| \le \|u - q\| \text{ for all } q \in Z \}, $$ i.e., the set of points of $V$ that have $f$ as a closest point in $Z$.

Voronoi cells are convex, being intersections of half-spaces. Let $Z \subseteq V$ be an irreducible variety of dimension $m$. If $f \in Z$ is smooth, then $\mathrm{Vor}_Z(f)$ is a full-dimensional subset of the normal space $f + (T_f Z)^\perp$: it is contained in it by the first-order optimality condition, and contains a neighborhood of $f$ in it, since $Z$ is a manifold near $f$ (Cifuentes et al., 2022). Thus its dimension is the codimension $N - m$ of $Z$. At a singular point, instead, the Voronoi cell can be larger. For instance, the Voronoi cell of the cusp of the plane curve $y^2 = x^3$ is two-dimensional, while those of its smooth points are one-dimensional. It can also be smaller: the Voronoi cell of a node of a plane curve, where two branches cross, is the node itself.

Since learning is, ideally, distance minimization (see Learning as distance optimization), this can be interpreted as an implicit bias towards singularities. Indeed, $f \in \M$ minimizes the ideal loss $\|f - f^*\|^2$ over $\M$ precisely when $f^* \in \mathrm{Vor}_{\M}(f)$. Hence a singular point with a large Voronoi cell is more likely to be a global minimum than a given smooth point, in the sense that it minimizes the distance for a larger set of target functions $f^*$, namely those in its Voronoi cell. If this cell is full-dimensional, as at the cusp above, it even has positive measure, whereas the Voronoi cell of a smooth point lies in its normal space, which has measure zero.

Relation to subnetworks

In neural networks, the singularities of $\M$ often arise from subnetworks (). The relation between subnetworks and singularities is intuitive: both are degenerate objects, from the neural and the geometric perspective, respectively. For MLPs, the link goes through the differential $d\varphi_\theta$, whose image consists of tangent vectors in $T_{f_\theta} \overline{\M}$. For subnetworks, as shown below, as $\theta$ varies in the fiber $\varphi^{-1}(f_\theta)$, these images span a space of dimension larger than $\dim \M$ (cf. Shahverdi et al., 2026, Theorem 4.2).

Theorem Let $\K$ have characteristic $0$, let $\sigma \in \K[t]$ have degree $r \ge 1$, and consider an MLP (). Let $0 < j < L$, let $e = \min\{d_0, \ldots, d_{j-1}\}$, and let $N = \binom{e + r}{r}$, multiplied by $\dout$ if $j = L - 1$. If $\dim \M < N$, then $f_\theta$ is a singular point of $\overline{\M}$ for every $\theta$ at which some neuron of layer $j$ is inactive or redundant. In particular, if $r \ge \dim \M$, the functions of all subnetworks are singular points of $\overline{\M}$.

Proof.

If the $i$-th neuron of layer $j$ is inactive or redundant, then $f_\theta$ is also computed by a parameter at which its outgoing weights $W_{j+1}[:,i]$ vanish: if $W_j[i,:] = 0$, its constant output $\sigma(b_j[i])$ can be absorbed by $b_{j+1}$, and if it is redundant, its output is $c$ times that of the $k$-th neuron, where $\sigma(\lambda t) = c\, \sigma(t)$, which can be absorbed by $W_{j+1}[:,k]$. Since the singular locus of $\overline{\M}$ is Zariski closed, it then suffices to consider a generic $\theta$ in the linear subspace $S \subseteq \Theta$ where $W_{j+1}[:,i] = 0$.

For $\theta \in S$, the neuron does not affect $f_\theta$: replacing its incoming weights and bias by any $u \in \K^{d_{j-1}}$ and $b \in \K$ yields a parameter in the fiber of $f_\theta$, at which the derivative of the output with respect to $W_{j+1}[k,i]$ is $\lambda_k(x)\, \sigma(u \cdot h_\theta(x) + b)$. Here, $h_\theta \colon \K^{\din} \to \K^{d_{j-1}}$ is computed by the first $j - 1$ layers ($h_\theta(x) = x$ if $j = 1$), and $\lambda_k$, the derivative of the output with respect to the $k$-th pre-activation of layer $j + 1$, does not depend on $(u, b)$. Hence, $T_{f_\theta} \overline{\M}$ contains $\lambda_k E_\theta$, where $E_\theta$ is the span of the functions $\sigma(u \cdot h_\theta(x) + b)$. If $j = L - 1$, $\lambda_k$ is the $k$-th standard basis vector of $\K^{\dout}$, so that $T_{f_\theta} \overline{\M}$ contains $E_\theta^{\dout}$. Otherwise, $\lambda_k$ is a product of the matrices $W_L, \ldots, W_{j+2}$ and of diagonal matrices with entries $\sigma'(\cdot)$, applied to the $k$-th standard basis vector, hence nonzero for generic $\theta$, and multiplication by $\lambda_k$ is injective, since $\K[x]$ is a domain. In both cases, $\dim T_{f_\theta} \overline{\M} \ge N > \dim \M$ as soon as $\dim E_\theta \ge \binom{e + r}{r}$.

By , with $t = u \cdot h_\theta(x)$, $E_\theta$ contains the powers $(u \cdot h_\theta(x))^s$ for $s \le r$, hence, by the polarization identity in the proof of , all the polynomials of degree at most $r$ in $h_\theta(x)$. For generic $\theta$, the Jacobian matrix of $h_\theta$, a product of the matrices $W_{j-1}, \ldots, W_1$ and of diagonal matrices with entries $\sigma'(\cdot)$, has rank $e$ at generic $x$, so that, up to relabeling, the first $e$ coordinates $h_1, \ldots, h_e$ of $h_\theta$ have a Jacobian matrix $J$ of rank $e$. Then no nonzero polynomial $q$ satisfies $q(h_1, \ldots, h_e) = 0$: otherwise, for such a $q$ of minimal degree, the chain rule gives $(\nabla q)(h_1, \ldots, h_e)\, J = 0$, hence $(\nabla q)(h_1, \ldots, h_e) = 0$, so that $\nabla q = 0$ by minimality, and $q$ is a nonzero constant. Thus, the $\binom{e + r}{r}$ monomials of degree at most $r$ in $h_1, \ldots, h_e$ are linearly independent elements of $E_\theta$. Finally, $N \ge \binom{1 + r}{r} = r + 1$ gives the last claim.

The converse is believed to hold, under appropriate assumptions on $\sigma$, e.g., that it is generic and of large enough degree. This would imply that singular points of $\overline{\M}$ that lie in $\M$ are precisely the functions of subnetworks. This is still open (Shahverdi et al., 2026).

However, singular points do not necessarily have larger Voronoi cells, and it has been shown that, for MLPs, those of subnetworks usually do not: for shallow MLPs, and for deep ones under some assumptions, the Voronoi cell of the function of a subnetwork with inactive neurons is reduced to the function itself, as at the node above (Ribot et al., 2026). For ResNets (), instead, subnetworks are conjectured to have larger Voronoi cells. As argued above, this would provide a geometric explanation of the bias towards subnetworks (see Submodels). In any case, this bias can also be argued dynamically, as discussed in Feature learning.

Gradient flow

As we have seen in Learning as distance optimization, a model learns by minimizing a loss $L$ over $\M$ through its parameters, i.e., by minimizing $L \circ \varphi$ over $\Theta$. We now look at the dynamics of this process. Consider an algebraic model over $\K = \R$, fix a scalar product on $V$ and one on $\Theta = \R^{\dpar}$ (e.g., the standard one), and let $L \colon V \to \R$ be differentiable, e.g., the MSE. Gradients are taken with respect to these scalar products, and $d\varphi_\theta^* \colon V \to \Theta$ denotes the adjoint of the differential $d\varphi_\theta \colon \Theta \to V$ of the parametrization map at $\theta \in \Theta$.

Definition (Gradient flow). The gradient flow of the model for the loss $L$ is the differential equation $$ \dot\theta(t) = -\nabla (L \circ \varphi)(\theta(t)) $$ on $\Theta$. Its solutions $t \mapsto \theta(t)$ are called trajectories, and $f(t) = f_{\theta(t)}$ is the corresponding trajectory in $\M$.

In practice, the gradient flow is usually discretized, via the Euler method, as $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\, \nabla (L \circ \varphi)(\theta_t)$, where $\eta > 0$ is the learning rate. This is gradient descent.

By the chain rule, the differential of $L \circ \varphi$ factors as $d(L \circ \varphi)_\theta = dL_{f_\theta} \circ d\varphi_\theta$, i.e., $\nabla (L \circ \varphi)(\theta) = d\varphi_\theta^* \nabla L(f_\theta)$. Since $\dot f = d\varphi_\theta\, \dot\theta$, this implies that, along the trajectories of the gradient flow, \begin{equation}\label{eq:function-space-flow} \dot f(t) = -K_{\theta(t)} \nabla L(f(t)), \qquad K_\theta = d\varphi_\theta\, d\varphi_\theta^* \colon V \to V. \end{equation}

The operator $K_\theta$, which is positive semi-definite with the same image as $d\varphi_\theta$, is the neural tangent kernel (NTK) of the model at $\theta$ (Jacot et al., 2018). Geometrically, $K_\theta$ is the push-forward along $d\varphi_\theta$ of the Euclidean cometric of $\Theta$, i.e., of the inverse of its scalar product. However, $K_\theta$ depends on $\theta$, and not only on $f_\theta$, so these push-forwards do not, in general, define a cometric on $\M$: technically, $\theta \mapsto K_\theta$ is a field of positive semi-definite quadratic forms on the pull-back bundle $\varphi^* T^*V$. The rank of $K_\theta$ equals that of $d\varphi_\theta$, which is $\dim \M$ for generic $\theta$ (see Identifiability). The points $\theta$ at which it is smaller are, by definition, the critical points of $\varphi$; when $\dpar = \dim \M$, i.e., when the generic fiber is finite, they are also called ramification points.

Since $\operatorname{im} d\varphi_\theta^* = (\ker d\varphi_\theta)^\perp$, the gradient flow is orthogonal to $\ker d\varphi_\theta$ at every $\theta \in \Theta$. Consequently, whatever the loss, a closed submanifold of $\Theta$ whose tangent spaces contain $(\ker d\varphi_\theta)^\perp$, i.e., whose normal spaces lie in $\ker d\varphi_\theta$, is invariant to the gradient flow: trajectories never leave it. The kernel of the differential $d\varphi_\theta$ therefore plays a fundamental role in the training dynamics. Since $\varphi$ is constant on its fibers, their tangent spaces lie in $\ker d\varphi_\theta$, with equality for generic $\theta$; in particular, the gradient flow is orthogonal to the fibers. From the perspective of algebraic geometry, $\ker d\varphi_\theta$ is precisely the (Zariski) tangent space at $\theta$ to the scheme-theoretic fiber, i.e., to the subscheme of $\Theta$ cut out by the equations $\varphi = f_\theta$. The two tangent spaces coincide when the fiber is reduced, but not in general. Thus, describing $\ker d\varphi_\theta$ is, in a sense, an identifiability problem.

A natural question is whether trajectories converge, and how fast. For analytic models, including algebraic ones, it is well-known that the only alternative to convergence is escaping to infinity (Łojasiewicz, 1984). For algebraic models, the rate of convergence is bounded in terms of the degree and, at parameters computing the target, of the multiplicity of the fiber.

Theorem (Convergence). Let $F = L \circ \varphi$, and assume that it is real analytic and bounded below. Then every trajectory of the gradient flow extends to all $t \ge 0$, and either $\|\theta(t)\| \to \infty$ as $t \to \infty$, or $\theta(t)$ converges to a critical point $\theta_\infty$ of $F$.

Moreover, assume that $\theta(t)$ converges, the model is algebraic, $L$ is a polynomial with $d = \deg F \ge 2$.

  • If $\dpar \ge 2$ then $$ F(\theta(t)) - F(\theta_\infty) = O\big(t^{-R/(R - 2)}\big), \qquad \|\theta(t) - \theta_\infty\| = O\big(t^{-1/(R - 2)}\big), $$ where $R = d\, (3d - 3)^{\dpar - 1}$.
  • If $L(f) = \|f - f^*\|^2$ for some $f^* \in V$, and $\theta_\infty$ is an isolated point, of multiplicity $\mu$, of the complex fiber of $\varphi$, then $$F(\theta(t)) = O\big(t^{-\mu/(\mu - 1)}\big), \qquad \|\theta(t) - \theta_\infty\| = O\big(t^{-1/(2\mu - 2)}\big)$$ if $\mu \ge 2$, and both decay exponentially if $\mu = 1$.

Proof.

Along a trajectory, $\frac{d}{dt} F(\theta(t)) = -\|\dot\theta(t)\|^2$, so that, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $\|\theta(t) - \theta(0)\|^2 \le t \int_0^t \|\dot\theta(s)\|^2\, ds \le t\, \big(F(\theta(0)) - \inf F\big)$: trajectories do not blow up in finite time. Assume that $\|\theta(t)\|$ does not tend to $\infty$. Then $\theta(t_n) \to \theta_\infty$ for some $\theta_\infty \in \Theta$ and some $t_n \to \infty$, and $e(t) = F(\theta(t)) - F(\theta_\infty)$ decreases to $0$. If $e(t) = 0$ for some $t$, the trajectory is constant from then on, and all claims follow. Otherwise, by the Łojasiewicz gradient inequality (Łojasiewicz, 1963), there are $\delta, c > 0$ and $\rho \in [\tfrac{1}{2}, 1)$ such that $\|\nabla F(\theta)\| \ge c\, |F(\theta) - F(\theta_\infty)|^\rho$ whenever $\|\theta - \theta_\infty\| \lt \delta$. As long as $\theta(t)$ stays in this ball, $$ \frac{d}{dt} e(t)^{1 - \rho} = -(1 - \rho)\, e(t)^{-\rho}\, \|\nabla F(\theta(t))\|\, \|\dot\theta(t)\| \le -c\, (1 - \rho)\, \|\dot\theta(t)\|. $$ Choose $n$ such that $\|\theta(t_n) - \theta_\infty\| \lt \delta/2$ and $e(t_n)^{1 - \rho} \lt c\, (1 - \rho)\, \delta/2$. By the above, the length of the trajectory after $t_n$ is less than $\delta/2$, as long as it stays in the ball. Hence it never leaves the ball, it has finite length, and it converges, necessarily to $\theta_\infty$. Moreover, $\nabla F(\theta_\infty) = 0$, since otherwise $F(\theta(t))$ would decrease without bound.

For large $t$, integrating the inequality above over $[t, \infty)$ gives $\|\theta(t) - \theta_\infty\| \le e(t)^{1 - \rho} / \big(c\, (1 - \rho)\big)$. Moreover, $\dot e = -\|\nabla F(\theta)\|^2 \le -c^2 e^{2\rho}$, so that $e(t) = O\big(\exp(-c^2 t)\big)$ if $\rho = \tfrac{1}{2}$, and $e(t) = O\big(t^{-1/(2\rho - 1)}\big)$ if $\rho > \tfrac{1}{2}$. It remains to show that the gradient inequality holds near $\theta_\infty$ with $\rho = 1 - \frac{1}{R}$ in the first case, and $\rho = 1 - \frac{1}{2\mu}$ in the second. In the first case, this is the bound of D'Acunto and Kurdyka (2005) on the Łojasiewicz exponent of a polynomial of degree $d$ in $\dpar$ variables at a critical point, applied to $F - F(\theta_\infty)$.

In the second case, write $\varphi - f^* = (g_1, \ldots, g_N)$ in orthonormal coordinates on $V$, so that $F = \|g\|^2$ and $F(\theta_\infty) = 0$. Let $\mathcal{O}$ be the local ring of $\C^{\dpar}$ at $\theta_\infty$, with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$, and $I = (g_1, \ldots, g_N) \subseteq \mathcal{O}$; by definition, $\mu = \dim_\C \mathcal{O}/I$. By Nakayama's lemma, the images of the powers of $\mathfrak{m}$ in $\mathcal{O}/I$ decrease strictly until they vanish, hence $\mathfrak{m}^\mu \subseteq I$. Thus each $(\theta_i - \theta_{\infty, i})^\mu$ is a combination of the $g_l$ with coefficients in $\mathcal{O}$, which are bounded near $\theta_\infty$, and $\|\theta - \theta_\infty\|^\mu \le C\, \|g(\theta)\| = C\, F(\theta)^{1/2}$ for real $\theta$ near $\theta_\infty$. Combined with the inequality $\|\theta - \theta_\infty\|\, \|\nabla F(\theta)\| \ge c\, F(\theta)$ near $\theta_\infty$ (Bochnak and Łojasiewicz, 1971), this gives $\|\nabla F\| \ge c'\, F^{1 - 1/(2\mu)}$.

The multiplicity $\mu$ in depends only on $\varphi$ and $f^*$, and $\mu \le \deg \varphi$: for generic linear forms $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_{\dpar}$ on $V$, $(\theta_\infty, f^*)$ is an isolated point of multiplicity at least $\mu$ of the intersection of $\Gamma_\varphi$ with $\{(\theta, f) : \lambda_j(f - f^*) = 0 \text{ for all } j\}$, so that the bound follows from the refined Bézout theorem (Fulton, 1998, §12.3), as in the proof of .

Conserved quantities

Quantities that do not change during training are a basic tool to analyze its dynamics. They confine the trajectories to subsets of $\Theta$ determined by the initialization, reducing the effective dimension of the dynamics, and influencing the solution eventually reached.

Definition (Conserved quantity). A conserved quantity of the model is a differentiable function $Q \colon \Theta \to \R$ such that $\nabla Q(\theta) \in \ker d\varphi_\theta$ for all $\theta \in \Theta$.

Since $\frac{d}{dt} Q(\theta(t)) = -\big\langle d\varphi_{\theta(t)} \nabla Q(\theta(t)), \nabla L(f(t)) \big\rangle$ along a trajectory, and $\nabla L = u$ is arbitrary for linear losses $L = \langle u, \cdot \rangle$, a differentiable $Q$ is a conserved quantity if and only if it is constant along every trajectory of the gradient flow, for every differentiable loss $L$ (Marcotte et al., 2023).

Since $\ker d\varphi_\theta$ is the tangent space of the fiber for generic $\theta$, non-constant conserved quantities exist only if the generic fiber is positive-dimensional, i.e., if $\dim \M < \dpar$. Such fibers often come from continuous parameter symmetries, whose orbits lie in the fibers (as in ), and some of these symmetries yield conserved quantities.

Let $G$ be a Lie group acting linearly on $\Theta$. For $\xi$ in its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, the infinitesimal action $\theta \mapsto \xi \cdot \theta = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t = 0} \exp(t\xi) \cdot \theta$ is a linear operator on $\Theta$. Let $\mathfrak{p} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$ be the subspace of the $\xi$ for which this operator is self-adjoint, and let $\mu \colon \Theta \to \mathfrak{p}^*$ be the quadratic map defined by $\mu(\theta)(\xi) = \mu_\xi(\theta) = \frac{1}{2} \langle \xi \cdot \theta, \theta \rangle$.

Theorem (Gradient map). If $f_{g \cdot \theta} = f_\theta$ for all $g \in G$ and $\theta \in \Theta$, then, for every $\xi \in \mathfrak{p}$, $\mu_\xi$ is a conserved quantity, with $\nabla \mu_\xi(\theta) = \xi \cdot \theta$. Hence, whatever the loss, every trajectory of the gradient flow stays in the real algebraic variety $\mu^{-1}(\mu(\theta(0)))$.

Proof.

Let $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $\theta \in \Theta$. Differentiating $\varphi(\exp(t\xi) \cdot \theta) = \varphi(\theta)$ at $t = 0$ gives $d\varphi_\theta(\xi \cdot \theta) = 0$. If $\xi \in \mathfrak{p}$, then, by self-adjointness, $d(\mu_\xi)_\theta(v) = \frac{1}{2} \langle \xi \cdot v, \theta \rangle + \frac{1}{2} \langle \xi \cdot \theta, v \rangle = \langle \xi \cdot \theta, v \rangle$ for all $v \in \Theta$, i.e., $\nabla \mu_\xi(\theta) = \xi \cdot \theta \in \ker d\varphi_\theta$. The last claim follows, as conserved quantities are constant along trajectories.

is an analogue of Noether's theorem for gradient flows (Kunin et al., 2021), with the scalar product in place of the symplectic form. Accordingly, $\mu$ is the analogue of a moment map, called a gradient map (Heinzner et al., 2008).

Example (Balanced weights). For a linear network (), $f_\theta$ is invariant under the action of $\mathrm{GL}_{d_1}(\R) \times \cdots \times \mathrm{GL}_{d_{L-1}}(\R)$ by $W_j \mapsto g_j W_j g_{j-1}^{-1}$, where $g_0$ and $g_L$ are identity matrices. By , the matrices $W_j W_j^\top - W_{j+1}^\top W_{j+1}$, for $0 \lt j \lt L$, are conserved. For a deep monomial network, i.e., an MLP with $\sigma(t) = t^r$ and vanishing biases, $f_\theta$ is invariant under the action of invertible diagonal matrices $D_1, \ldots, D_{L-1}$ by $W_j \mapsto D_j W_j D_{j-1}^{-r}$, where $D_0$ and $D_L$ are identity matrices. By , the quantities $\|W_j[i,:]\|^2 - r\, \|W_{j+1}[:,i]\|^2$, one for each hidden neuron, are conserved. Parameters at which these conserved quantities vanish are called balanced.

Feature learning and limiting dynamics

The kernel of $d\varphi_\theta$ (see Gradient flow) also governs how, and when, the neurons of a network activate during training.

Subnetworks () provide examples where $\ker d\varphi_\theta$ is larger than the tangent space of the fiber. Let $c \in \R$ be a critical point of $\sigma$ of order $k \ge 2$, i.e., $\sigma'(c) = \cdots = \sigma^{(k-1)}(c) = 0 \neq \sigma^{(k)}(c)$. For an MLP (), possibly with skip connections (), let $n = (j, i)$ be the $i$-th neuron of a layer $j$ with activation $\sigma$, and let $S \subseteq \Theta$ be the affine subspace defined by $W_j[i,:] = 0$ and $b_j[i] = c$. For $\theta \in S$, the neuron $n$ is inactive, with pre-activation constantly equal to $c$. Since $\sigma'(c) = 0$, the derivatives of $f_\theta$ with respect to $W_j[i,:]$ and $b_j[i]$ vanish, so that the normal spaces of $S$ lie in $\ker d\varphi_\theta$. By the above, $S$ is invariant under the gradient flow, for every loss. Yet perturbing $W_j[i,:]$ by $\varepsilon$ changes $f_\theta$ at order $\varepsilon^k$, and, in general, these $d_{j-1}$ directions are not tangent to the fiber: the scheme-theoretic fiber is non-reduced.

The order $k$ measures how flat the loss is near $S$. Let $w_n = (W_j[i,:], b_j[i] - c)$, so that $S = \{w_n = 0\}$, and let $\theta^{\setminus n}$ be $\theta$ with $w_n$ replaced by $0$. Then $f_\theta = \Phi_j\big(\bar h_j + \sigma(c + \langle w_n, \hat h_{j-1} \rangle)\, e_i\big)$, where $h_j$ is the output of layer $j$, $\hat h = (h, 1)$, $\bar h_j$ is $h_j$ without the contribution of $n$, and $\Phi_j$ is the map computed by the subsequent layers, none of which depends on $w_n$. Since $t^k$ divides $\sigma(c + t) - \sigma(c)$, every monomial of $f_\theta - f_{\theta^{\setminus n}}$ in $w_n$ has degree at least $k$. Hence, for every smooth loss $L \colon V \to \R$, $F = L \circ \varphi$ satisfies $$ \partial^\beta_{w_n} F = 0 \ \text{ on } S \qquad \text{for every } 1 \le |\beta| \le k - 1, \tag{H} $$ and $F(\theta) = F(\theta^{\setminus n}) - U_n(\theta; w_n) + O(\|w_n\|^{k+1})$, where the utility $U_n(\theta; \cdot)$ of $n$ at $\theta$ is a $k$-form, namely minus the homogeneous part of degree $k$ of the Taylor expansion of $F$ in $w_n$ at $\theta^{\setminus n}$. For the MSE, the chain rule gives, for $\theta \in S$, $$ U_n(\theta; w) = 2c_\sigma\, \mathbb{E}\big[\langle w, \hat h_{j-1} \rangle^k\, \langle d\Phi_j(h_j)\, e_i,\, y - f_\theta(x) \rangle\big], \qquad c_\sigma = \frac{\sigma^{(k)}(c)}{k!}, $$ where $\mathbb{E}$ is the mean over the datapoints $(x, y)$: the correlation between the $k$-th power of the change $\langle w, \hat h_{j-1} \rangle$ of the pre-activation of the neuron and the back-propagated residual. In particular, at distance $\varepsilon$ from $S$, the normal component of the gradient of $F$ is $O(\varepsilon^{k-1})$. Hence, trajectories starting near $S$ move almost parallel to it, essentially following the dynamics of the network without this neuron, but they may escape. If the loss decreases away from $S$, the distance $\delta$ from $S$ grows according to $\dot\delta \propto \delta^{k-1}$, so that escaping from distance $\varepsilon$ takes a time of order $\log(1/\varepsilon)$ if $k = 2$, and of order $\varepsilon^{2-k}$ if $k \ge 3$.

This mechanism is a starting point for a theory of feature learning and sparsity. Consider initializations at scale $\alpha \to 0$ around a parameter at which all the neurons are inactive in this way, e.g., with vanishing weights and hidden biases equal to $c$. Learning is then expected to proceed sequentially, through a saddle-to-saddle dynamics (Jacot et al., 2021; Kunin et al., 2025). During a plateau, the loss is almost constant, and the inactive neurons move almost parallel to their subspaces. Their weights stay small, but align with the directions along which activating them would decrease the loss the most, while accumulating this utility over time. Eventually, a neuron escapes: it suddenly activates, and the loss drops abruptly. Metaphorically, the neuron learns a feature. The active neurons then settle at a critical point of the loss of the subnetwork they form, which is a saddle of the full loss, and a new plateau begins, until convergence. Thus the trajectory visits a sequence of subnetworks, activating neurons only when needed, which biases learning towards sparse networks. All of this is an infinitesimal phenomenon: as $\alpha \to 0$, with time rescaled by the escape time, the trajectory degenerates into plateaus separated by instantaneous jumps between saddles, and the loss curve becomes piecewise constant. The limit has been proved only in special cases, such as diagonal linear networks (Pesme and Flammarion, 2023) and, as we show below, polynomial ResNets.

Convergence proof for ResNets

We now prove the saddle-to-saddle limit for polynomial ResNets () when $k \ge 3$. A ResNet of width $d$ and depth $L$ computes $f_\theta = h_L$, where $h_0 = x$ and $h_j = h_{j-1} + \sigma(W_j h_{j-1} + b_j)$. Since the skip connections play the role of outgoing weights, the $w_n$, for its neurons $n = (j, i)$, form coordinates in which $\Theta = (\R^{d+1})^{Ld}$ and $\theta = 0$ is the parameter at which all the neurons are inactive. Let $\iota_n \colon \R^{d+1} \to \Theta$ be the inclusion of the $n$-th factor. For a set $A$ of neurons, regarded as active, let $S_A = \{\theta : w_n = 0 \text{ for all } n \notin A\}$, let $\pi_A$ be the orthogonal projection onto $S_A$, and let $F_A = F|_{S_A}$. As the intersection of the subspaces $S$ of the neurons not in $A$, $S_A$ is invariant under the gradient flow, which on $S_A$ is the gradient flow of $F_A$. Hence the critical points of $F_A$ are critical points of $F$, and those at which some $U_n(\theta; \cdot)$, $n \notin A$, takes a positive value are not local minima of $F$. Besides smoothness, the proof below only uses (H), for every neuron $n$, and therefore applies to every smooth function on $(\R^{d+1})^{Ld}$ satisfying it. Let $\theta^\alpha = (w^\alpha_n)_n$ be the trajectory of the gradient flow with $\theta^\alpha(0) = \alpha \xi$, for a fixed $\xi \in \Theta$.

Let $\theta^*$ be a non-degenerate local minimum of $F_A$, let $n \notin A$, and let $u^*$ be a non-degenerate local maximum of $U_n(\theta^*; \cdot)|_{\mathbb{S}^d}$ (in the Riemannian sense on the unit sphere $\mathbb{S}^d \subseteq \R^{d+1}$) with $U_n(\theta^*; u^*) > 0$. The escape orbit of $\theta^*$ along $(n, u^*)$ is the maximal trajectory $\gamma$ of the gradient flow in $S_{A \cup \{n\}}$ such that $\gamma(t) \to \theta^*$ and $\gamma_n(t)/\|\gamma_n(t)\| \to u^*$ as $t \to -\infty$, where $\gamma_n$ is the $n$-th component of $\gamma$. As shown in the proof of , it exists and is unique up to time translation, and it is the limit, as $\varepsilon \to 0$, of the trajectories starting at $\theta^* + \iota_n(\varepsilon u^*)$, suitably shifted in time.

Definition (Saddle-to-saddle process). Set $s_0 = 0$, $A_0 = \emptyset$, $\theta^{(0)} = 0$ and $\Xi_n(0) = \xi_n$ for every neuron $n$. At stage $m \ge 0$, we are given $s_m$, a set $A_m$ of neurons, a non-degenerate local minimum $\theta^{(m)}$ of $F_{A_m}$, and $\Xi_n(s_m) \in \R^{d+1}$ for $n \notin A_m$. Each dormant $\Xi_n$, $n \notin A_m$, solves \begin{equation}\label{eq:dormant} \frac{d}{ds} \Xi_n = \nabla U^{(m)}_n(\Xi_n), \qquad U^{(m)}_n = U_n(\theta^{(m)}; \cdot), \end{equation} up to its blow-up time $s_m + T^{(m)}_n \in (s_m, \infty]$. Let $s_{m+1} = s_m + \min_{n \notin A_m} T^{(m)}_n$. The stage is non-degenerate if $s_{m+1} < \infty$ and:

  • (ND1) the minimum is attained at a single neuron $n_{m+1}$;
  • (ND2) as $s \to s_{m+1}$, $\Xi_{n_{m+1}}(s)/\|\Xi_{n_{m+1}}(s)\|$ converges to a non-degenerate local maximum $u_{m+1}$ of $U^{(m)}_{n_{m+1}}|_{\mathbb{S}^d}$;
  • (ND3) the escape orbit $\gamma_{m+1}$ of $\theta^{(m)}$ along $(n_{m+1}, u_{m+1})$ is defined for all $t \ge 0$, and converges to a non-degenerate local minimum $\theta^{(m+1)}$ of $F_{A_{m+1}}$, where $A_{m+1} = A_m \cup \{n_{m+1}\}$.

In that case, the next stage starts from the values $\Xi_n(s_{m+1})$, $n \notin A_{m+1}$. Since every such stage activates a new neuron, there are at most $Ld$ of them. The limit path is $\bar\theta(s) = \theta^{(m)}$ for $s_m \le s < s_{m+1}$.

Writing $\Xi_n = R_n u_n$ with $\|u_n\| = 1$, Euler's identity turns \eqref{eq:dormant} into $$ \frac{d}{ds} R_n^{2-k} = -k(k-2)\, U^{(m)}_n(u_n), \qquad \frac{d}{ds} u_n = R_n^{k-2}\, \nabla_{\mathbb{S}} U^{(m)}_n(u_n), $$ where, for a $k$-form $U$, $\nabla_{\mathbb{S}} U(u) = \nabla U(u) - kU(u)\, u$ is the gradient of $U|_{\mathbb{S}^d}$. Thus $n$ activates when its accumulated utility $\int_0^s U^{(m(s'))}_n(u_n(s'))\, ds'$, where $m(s')$ is the stage at time $s'$, reaches $\|\xi_n\|^{2-k}/(k(k-2))$, while its direction performs gradient ascent of the utility on the sphere. This is the alternation of utility maximization and loss minimization of alternating gradient flows (Kunin et al., 2025). At a blow-up, $R_n^{2-k}$ tends to $0$ at a bounded rate, so that the time $\int R_n^{k-2}\, ds$ of the ascent diverges, and $U^{(m)}_n(u_n)$ increases to a limit, which is positive since $R_n^{2-k}$ vanishes. Hence $U^{(m)}_{n_{m+1}}(u_{m+1}) > 0$ in (ND2), as required by (ND3).

Theorem (Saddle-to-saddle limit). Assume that stages $0, \ldots, M - 1$ are non-degenerate, and let $\bar s \in (s_M, s_{M+1})$. For $\alpha$ small, $\theta^\alpha$ is defined on $[0, \alpha^{2-k} \bar s]$ and, as $\alpha \to 0$, in the rescaled time $s = \alpha^{k-2} t$:

  • (Plateaus) for every $\delta > 0$, $\theta^\alpha(\alpha^{2-k} s) \to \bar\theta(s)$ uniformly for $s \in [0, \bar s]$ outside the intervals $(s_m - \delta, s_m + \delta)$, $1 \le m \le M$; in particular, the loss converges to the step function $F(\bar\theta(s))$, and $F(\theta^{(0)}) > \cdots > F(\theta^{(M)})$;
  • (Dormant neurons) $\alpha^{-1} w^\alpha_n(\alpha^{2-k} s) \to \Xi_n(s)$ uniformly on $[0, \bar s]$ for $n \notin A_M$, and on $[0, s_m - \delta]$ for $n = n_m$ and every $\delta > 0$;
  • (Jumps) for $1 \le m \le M$, there are times $t^\alpha_m$ with $\alpha^{k-2} t^\alpha_m \to s_m$ such that $\theta^\alpha(t^\alpha_m + \cdot)$ converges to a time translate of $\gamma_m$, uniformly on compact sets; moreover, if $\delta$ is smaller than half the gaps between $0, s_1, \ldots, s_M, \bar s$, the curve $\{\theta^\alpha(\alpha^{2-k} s) : |s - s_m| \le \delta\}$ converges to the closure of $\gamma_m(\R)$ in the Hausdorff distance.

Each $\theta^{(m)}$ is a critical point of $F$ and, for $m < M$, a saddle, i.e., not a local minimum.

In words, during the $m$-th plateau, which lasts a time of order $\alpha^{2-k}$, the active parameters stay near the saddle $\theta^{(m)}$, while the weights of the inactive neurons, of order $\alpha$, follow the dormant flows. The first of these to blow up activates, and the parameters jump to $\theta^{(m+1)}$ along the escape orbit, in a vanishing rescaled time. The assumption $k \ge 3$ separates the two time scales, since $\alpha^{2-k} \to \infty$. It also makes the dormant flows blow up in finite time, at times that depend on $\xi$, which breaks the tie between the neurons $(1, i), \ldots, (L, i)$, whose utilities coincide at $\theta = 0$ if $\sigma(c) = 0$, since all the layers are then the identity. For $k = 2$, the dormant flows are linear, these neurons escape at once, and the outcome of the first jump depends on the ratios of their initial weights.

Proof.

Constants, including those in $O(\cdot)$, are uniform on the bounded sets involved, and $C$ may change from line to line. Throughout, $\theta^*$ is a non-degenerate local minimum of $F_A$, and $\mu > 0$ is such that $\nabla^2 F_A \succeq \mu$ near $\theta^*$.

Flatness. Derivatives of $F$ in directions tangent to $\{w_{n'} = 0\}$ still satisfy (H) in $w_{n'}$, so that, by Taylor's formula, they change by $O(\|w_{n'}\|^k)$ when $w_{n'}$ is set to $0$. Applying this to the neurons not in $A$ one at a time, and expanding $\nabla_{w_n} F$ to order $k - 1$ in $w_n$, we get, locally uniformly in $\theta$:

  • (F1) $\nabla_{w_n} F(\theta) = -\nabla U_n(\theta; w_n) + O(\|w_n\|^k)$, and in particular $\nabla_{w_n} F(\theta) = O(\|w_n\|^{k-1})$;
  • (F2) $\pi_A \nabla F(\theta) = \nabla F_A(\pi_A \theta) + O\big(\sum_{n \notin A} \|w_n\|^k\big)$.

Plateau. There is $r_0 > 0$ such that the following holds. Let $\Xi_n$, $n \notin A$, solve $\frac{d}{ds} \Xi_n = \nabla U_n(\theta^*; \Xi_n)$ on $[s', s'']$, and let $\tau_\alpha$ be times with $\|\pi_A \theta^\alpha(\tau_\alpha) - \theta^*\| \le r_0$ and $\alpha^{-1} w^\alpha_n(\tau_\alpha) \to \Xi_n(s')$ for $n \notin A$. Then, as $\alpha \to 0$, $\alpha^{-1} w^\alpha_n\big(\tau_\alpha + \alpha^{2-k}(s - s')\big) \to \Xi_n(s)$ uniformly on $[s', s'']$, for $n \notin A$, and, for small $\alpha$ and $0 \le t - \tau_\alpha \le \alpha^{2-k}(s'' - s')$, \begin{equation}\label{eq:plateau} \|\pi_A \theta^\alpha(t) - \theta^*\| \le e^{-\mu(t - \tau_\alpha)}\, \|\pi_A \theta^\alpha(\tau_\alpha) - \theta^*\| + O(\alpha^k). \end{equation} Indeed, as long as the rescaled weights $\alpha^{-1} w^\alpha_n$, $n \notin A$, stay bounded, $e = \pi_A \theta^\alpha - \theta^*$ satisfies $\dot e = -\nabla F_A(\theta^* + e) + O(\alpha^k)$ by (F2), and \eqref{eq:plateau} follows from the strong convexity of $F_A$ and Grönwall's lemma. In the rescaled time, (F1), the smoothness of $U_n$ in $\theta$ and its homogeneity in $w$ give $$ \frac{d}{ds}\big(\alpha^{-1} w^\alpha_n\big) = -\alpha^{1-k} \nabla_{w_n} F(\theta^\alpha) = \nabla U_n\big(\theta^*; \alpha^{-1} w^\alpha_n\big) + O\big(\|\pi_A \theta^\alpha - \theta^*\| + \alpha\big), $$ and, by \eqref{eq:plateau}, the error term has integral $O(\alpha^{k-2} + \alpha)$ over $[s', s'']$. Grönwall's lemma gives the convergence, and a continuity argument removes the a priori bound.

Escape. Let $n \notin A$, $A' = A \cup \{n\}$ and $U = U_n(\theta^*; \cdot)$, and let $u^*$ be a non-degenerate local maximum of $U|_{\mathbb{S}^d}$ with $U(u^*) > 0$. Write $\theta = a + \iota_n(\rho u) + z$, with $a = \pi_A \theta$, $\rho = \|w_n\|$, $\|u\| = 1$, and $z = \theta - \pi_{A'} \theta$, which collects the other inactive neurons. We show:

  • (E1) the escape orbit $\gamma$ of $\theta^*$ along $(n, u^*)$ exists and is unique up to time translation, and $\|\pi_A \gamma - \theta^*\| = O(\|\gamma_n\|^k)$ as $t \to -\infty$;
  • (E2) there are $\rho_1, r, \varepsilon_1, C > 0$ such that, if a trajectory satisfies $\rho_0 := \rho < \rho_1$, $\|u - u^*\| \le r$, $\eta := \|z\| \le \varepsilon_1 \rho_0$ and $\|a - \theta^*\| \le \rho_0$ at a time $t_0$, then $\rho$ increases and reaches $\rho_1$ at a time $t_1 \le t_0 + C\rho_0^{2-k}$, and $\|z\| \le 2\eta$ on $[t_0, t_1]$. Moreover, as $\rho_0 \to 0$, uniformly in the trajectory, $\theta(t_1) \to \gamma(0)$ and $\sup_{[t_0, t_1]} \operatorname{dist}\big(\theta, \gamma((-\infty, 0])\big) \to 0$, where $\gamma$ is normalized so that $\|\gamma_n\|$ first reaches $\rho_1$ at $t = 0$.

The escape is degenerate, since the linearization of the flow in $w_n$ vanishes. A centre manifold reduction removes the active coordinates and, in polar coordinates, the degeneracy can be divided out, which turns the escape orbit into the unstable manifold of a hyperbolic equilibrium. Below, $r$ is small, and then $\rho_1$ and $\varepsilon_1 \le 1$ are small.

Polar coordinates. By (H), $-\nabla_{w_n} F(a + \iota_n(\rho u) + z) = \rho^{k-1} G(\rho, u, a, z)$, with $G$ smooth for all $\rho \in \R$ and $G(0, u, a, z) = \nabla U_n(a + z; u)$. Hence the gradient flow reads \begin{equation}\label{eq:polar} \dot\rho = \rho^{k-1} \langle u, G \rangle, \qquad \dot u = \rho^{k-2} \big(G - \langle u, G \rangle\, u\big), \qquad \dot a = -\pi_A \nabla F(\theta). \end{equation} At $(\rho, a, z) = (0, \theta^*, 0)$, Euler's identity gives $\langle u, G \rangle = kU(u)$ and $G - \langle u, G \rangle\, u = \nabla_{\mathbb{S}} U(u)$, whose zero $u^*$ is a sink, with the Riemannian Hessian as linearization.

Invariance. Under the assumptions of (E2), a continuity argument gives the following on $[t_0, t_1]$. First, $\kappa \le \langle u, G \rangle \le C$ for some $\kappa > 0$, so that $\dot\rho \ge \kappa \rho^{k-1}$: $\rho$ increases, and $t_1 - t_0 \le C\rho_0^{2-k}$. Next, $\frac{d}{dt} \|z\|^{2-k} \ge -C$ by (F1), so that $\|z\|^{2-k} \ge \eta^{2-k} - C\rho_0^{2-k} \ge (2\eta)^{2-k}$, as $\eta \le \varepsilon_1 \rho_0$. Next, $\|u - u^*\| \le r$, since $\dot u$ points inwards where $\|u - u^*\| = r$: $\nabla_{\mathbb{S}} U(u)$ does, and $G - \langle u, G \rangle\, u - \nabla_{\mathbb{S}} U(u) = O(\rho + \|a - \theta^*\| + \|z\|)$ is small. Finally, (F2) and the strong convexity of $F_A$ give $\frac{d}{dt} \|a - \theta^*\| \le -\mu \|a - \theta^*\| + C(\rho^k + \|z\|^k)$, so that, as $\rho$ increases and $\|z\| \le 2\rho$, \begin{equation}\label{eq:slaving} \|a - \theta^*\| \le e^{-\mu(t - t_0)} \rho_0 + C\rho^k \qquad \text{on } [t_0, t_1]. \end{equation}

Centre manifold. By (H) and $k \ge 3$, the linearization at $\theta^*$ of the gradient flow of $F_{A'}$ is $-\nabla^2 F_A(\theta^*) \oplus 0$ on $S_{A'} = S_A \oplus \R^{d+1}$. By the centre manifold theorem (Carr, 1981, Chapter 2, Theorem 1), this flow has a locally invariant manifold $W^c = \{a = \psi(w_n)\}$ near $\theta^*$, where $\psi$ is of class $C^2$, $\psi(0) = \theta^*$ and $d\psi_0 = 0$. By invariance, $q = a - \psi(w_n)$ satisfies $\dot q = -\big(\nabla^2 F_A(\theta^*) + o(1)\big)\, q$ along trajectories in $S_{A'}$ near $\theta^*$. Hence such a trajectory lies in $W^c$ if it stays near $\theta^*$ for all $t \le t'$.

Blow-up. On $W^c$, \eqref{eq:polar} becomes $\dot\rho = \rho^{k-1} \langle u, \tilde G \rangle$ and $\dot u = \rho^{k-2} (\tilde G - \langle u, \tilde G \rangle\, u)$, where $\tilde G(\rho, u) = G(\rho, u, \psi(\rho u), 0)$. In the time $\tau$ with $d\tau = \rho^{k-2}\, dt$, this is the flow of the vector field $(\rho \langle u, \tilde G \rangle, \tilde G - \langle u, \tilde G \rangle\, u)$ on $\R \times \mathbb{S}^d$, which is of class $C^2$ near $(0, u^*)$ and preserves $\{\rho = 0\}$, where it is the gradient flow of $U|_{\mathbb{S}^d}$. Hence $(0, u^*)$ is a hyperbolic equilibrium: the eigenvalue $kU(u^*) > 0$ has an eigenvector transverse to $\{\rho = 0\}$, and the other eigenvalues, those of the Riemannian Hessian, are negative. By the unstable manifold theorem (Teschl, 2012, Theorems 9.4 and 9.5), the points whose backward orbit stays in a small neighbourhood $B$ of $(0, u^*)$ form a $C^1$ curve $\Gamma$ transverse to $\{\rho = 0\}$, and their backward orbits converge to $(0, u^*)$. So $\Gamma \cap \{\rho > 0\}$ is an arc of a single orbit, which, since $dt = \rho^{2-k}\, d\tau$, gives a trajectory $\gamma$ in $W^c$ with $\gamma \to \theta^*$ and $\gamma_n/\|\gamma_n\| \to u^*$ as $t \to -\infty$. Conversely, ($\star$) every trajectory in $S_{A'}$ with $0 < \rho \le \rho_1$, $\|u - u^*\| \le r$ and $\|a - \theta^*\| \le r$ for all $t \le t'$ is a time translate of $\gamma$. Indeed, it lies in $W^c$, and $\kappa\rho^{k-1} \le \dot\rho \le C\rho^{k-1}$ gives $\rho \to 0$ and $\tau \to -\infty$ as $t \to -\infty$, so that $(\rho, u)$ is a backward orbit in $B$, which lies in $\Gamma$.

Proof of (E1). Every escape orbit satisfies the assumptions of ($\star$) for $t' \ll 0$, which gives existence and uniqueness. As $\psi(w) - \theta^* = o(\|w\|)$, the invariance step applies to $\gamma$ at every $t_0 \ll 0$, with $\eta = 0$. Hence $\|\gamma_n\|$ increases until it reaches $\rho_1$, at $t = 0$ say, and \eqref{eq:slaving} with $t_0 \to -\infty$ gives $\|\pi_A \gamma - \theta^*\| \le C\|\gamma_n\|^k$.

Proof of (E2). The first claims are contained in the invariance step. For the others, let $\theta_\ell$ be trajectories as in (E2) with $\rho_0 \to 0$, shifted so that $t_1 = 0$. Then $t_0 \to -\infty$, since $\dot\rho \le C\rho^{k-1}$. By compactness and continuous dependence on initial conditions, a subsequence converges, uniformly on compact subsets of $(-\infty, 0]$, to a trajectory $\theta_\infty$, which, by the invariance step, satisfies the assumptions of ($\star$) with $t' = 0$, and whose $\rho$ increases to $\rho_1$ at $t = 0$. Hence $\theta_\infty = \gamma$ for every subsequence, and $\theta_\ell \to \gamma$ uniformly on every $[-T, 0]$. Finally, by \eqref{eq:slaving}, $\theta_\ell(t)$ is $O(\rho')$-close to $\theta^*$, a limit point of $\gamma$, as long as $\rho \le \rho'$, and $\rho \ge \rho'$ only for $t \ge -C(\rho')^{2-k}$. Let $\ell \to \infty$, and then $\rho' \to 0$. In particular, by continuous dependence on initial conditions, the trajectories starting at $\theta^* + \iota_n(\varepsilon u^*)$, shifted so that $\|w_n\|$ reaches $\rho_1$ at $t = 0$, converge to $\gamma$ uniformly on compact sets as $\varepsilon \to 0$.

Induction. We show by induction on $m \le M$ that there are times $\tau^\alpha_m$ with $\alpha^{k-2} \tau^\alpha_m \to s_m$, $\|\pi_{A_m} \theta^\alpha(\tau^\alpha_m) - \theta^{(m)}\| \le r_0$, with $r_0$ as in the plateau step at $\theta^{(m)}$, and $\alpha^{-1} w^\alpha_n(\tau^\alpha_m) \to \Xi_n(s_m)$ for $n \notin A_m$. For $m = 0$, take $\tau^\alpha_0 = 0$. Assume the claim for $m$. The plateau step, applied on $[s_m, s_{m+1} - \delta]$ if $m < M$, and on $[s_M, s'']$ with $\bar s < s'' < s_{M+1}$ if $m = M$, gives the claim on dormant neurons on this interval. Since the inactive weights are $O(\alpha)$, it also gives $\|\theta^\alpha - \theta^{(m)}\| \le e^{-\mu(t - \tau^\alpha_m)} r_0 + O(\alpha)$, which tends to $0$ uniformly for $s \ge s_m + \delta$, and even for $s \ge 0$ if $m = 0$, since $\pi_\emptyset \theta = 0$. This gives the claim on plateaus.

For the jump, let $m < M$, $n_* = n_{m+1}$ and $u_* = u_{m+1}$, and take $\rho_1, r, \varepsilon_1$ as in (E2) at $(A_m, \theta^{(m)}, n_*, u_*)$. By (ND2), $\|\Xi_{n_*}(s)\| \to \infty$, with direction converging to $u_*$, as $s \to s_{m+1}$, and, by (ND1), $\sum_{n \notin A_{m+1}} \|\Xi_n\|^2 \le R^2$ on $[s_m, s_{m+1}]$ for some $R$. Fix $\delta > 0$ small, so that $R_\delta = \|\Xi_{n_*}(s_{m+1} - \delta)\| \ge 2R/\varepsilon_1$ and $\Xi_{n_*}(s_{m+1} - \delta)/R_\delta$ is $r/2$-close to $u_*$, and let $t_0$ be the time corresponding to $s_{m+1} - \delta$. By the plateau step, the assumptions of (E2) hold at $t_0$ for small $\alpha$: $\rho_0 = \alpha(R_\delta + o(1))$, $\|u - u_*\| \le r$, $\eta \le \alpha(R + o(1)) \le \varepsilon_1 \rho_0$, and $\|a - \theta^{(m)}\| = O(\alpha^k) \le \rho_0$. Let $t^\alpha_{m+1} = t_1$ be the first time after $\tau^\alpha_m$ at which $\|w_{n_*}\| = \rho_1$, which does not depend on $\delta$. By (E2), $\theta^\alpha(t_1) \to \gamma_{m+1}(0)$, with $\gamma_{m+1}$ normalized as in (E2). The jump is fast, since $\alpha^{k-2}(t_1 - t_0) \le C\alpha^{k-2}\rho_0^{2-k} \to CR_\delta^{2-k}$. Moreover, $\|z\| \le 2\eta = O(\alpha)$ on $[t_0, t_1]$, so that, by (F1), the rescaled weights $\alpha^{-1} w^\alpha_n$ of the other dormant neurons have velocity $\alpha^{1-k} \|\nabla_{w_n} F\| = O(1)$ in the time $s$, and move by $O(R_\delta^{2-k})$, uniformly in $\delta$. By continuous dependence on initial conditions, $\theta^\alpha(t_1 + \cdot) \to \gamma_{m+1}$ uniformly on compact sets, which is the first claim on jumps. By (ND3), there is $T$ with $\|\gamma_{m+1}(T) - \theta^{(m+1)}\| \le r_0/2$. Set $\tau^\alpha_{m+1} = t_1 + T$. On $[t_1, t_1 + T]$, the rescaled dormant weights move by $O(\alpha^{k-2})$. Hence $\alpha^{k-2} t_1$ and $\alpha^{k-2} \tau^\alpha_{m+1}$ lie in $[s_{m+1} - \delta, s_{m+1} - \delta + CR_\delta^{2-k}] + o(1)$ and, for $n \notin A_{m+1}$, $$ \big\|\alpha^{-1} w^\alpha_n(\tau^\alpha_{m+1}) - \Xi_n(s_{m+1})\big\| \le CR_\delta^{2-k} + \operatorname{osc}_{[s_{m+1} - \delta,\, s_{m+1}]} \Xi_n + o(1). $$ Since $t_1$ and $\tau^\alpha_{m+1}$ do not depend on $\delta$, and $R_\delta \to \infty$ as $\delta \to 0$, this proves the claim for $m + 1$, and $\alpha^{k-2} t^\alpha_{m+1} \to s_{m+1}$.

The plateau and jump steps cover $[0, \alpha^{2-k} \bar s]$, on which $\theta^\alpha$ stays bounded, hence is defined. For the Hausdorff convergence at $s_{m+1}$, run the jump step with some $\delta' < \delta$. Before $t_0$, the curve is close to $\theta^{(m)}$, by the claim on plateaus. On $[t_0, t_1]$, it is close to $\gamma_{m+1}((-\infty, 0])$, by (E2), and on $[t_1, t_1 + T]$ to $\gamma_{m+1}([0, T])$. Afterwards, it stays within $\|\gamma_{m+1}(T) - \theta^{(m+1)}\| + o(1)$ of $\theta^{(m+1)}$, by \eqref{eq:plateau}, and $T$ is arbitrarily large. Conversely, the curve approaches every point of the closure of $\gamma_{m+1}(\R)$, by the convergence of $\theta^\alpha(t_1 + \cdot)$ and the claim on plateaus at $s_{m+1} \pm \delta$. Finally, $F(\theta^{(m)}) > F(\theta^{(m+1)})$, since $F$ decreases strictly along $\gamma_{m+1}$, and $\theta^{(m)}$ is a critical point of $F_{A_m}$, hence of $F$, which, for $m < M$, is not a local minimum, since $U^{(m)}_{n_{m+1}}(u_{m+1}) > 0$.
